Free chlorine vs total chlorine is the distinction that decides whether your
testing is worth doing. Most pool owners read one number, treat it as "the
chlorine," and act on it. Sometimes that number is the right one. Often it's the
one that hides the problem.

There are three, they measure different things, and only one of them tells you
what to do today.

## What each number actually is

**Free chlorine (FC)** is chlorine still available to sanitize. It hasn't
reacted with anything yet. This is the working number — the one that determines
whether the pool is safe to swim in right now.

**Combined chlorine (CC)** is chlorine that has already reacted with ammonia and
nitrogen compounds — sweat, urine, sunscreen, leaf debris. These compounds are
called chloramines. They still register as chlorine on a test, but their
sanitizing power is a small fraction of free chlorine's, and they're what
produces the sharp "pool smell" and stinging eyes.

**Total chlorine (TC)** is free plus combined. It's not a separate substance,
just the sum. On its own it's the least useful of the three, which is unfortunate
because it's the one most commonly reported.

The relationship is arithmetic:

```
Total chlorine − Free chlorine = Combined chlorine
```

That subtraction is the actual diagnostic. A pool at 3 ppm total could be 3 free
and 0 combined, which is healthy — or 0.2 free and 2.8 combined, which is a pool
with essentially no sanitizer and a chloramine problem. Same total. Opposite
situations.

## Target ranges

CDC publishes **different figures for home pools and public facilities**, and
this is where most pool content goes wrong. The widely repeated numbers on other
sites are the public-facility standards, quoted at homeowners by people who
haven't noticed there are two sets.

| Measurement | CDC home pool | CDC public facility |
|---|---|---|
| Free chlorine | at least **1 ppm** — at least **2 ppm** where cyanuric acid is present | at least 2 ppm |
| pH | **7.0 – 7.8** | 7.2 – 7.8 |
| Combined chlorine | as close to zero as you can hold it | same |

Use the home figures. They're the ones written for your pool.

That said, the CDC minimum is a floor, not a target. A pool sitting at exactly
1 ppm is one hot afternoon away from zero, and zero is where algae starts. In
practice most owners run comfortably above the minimum so there's margin for a
day they don't test.

The number that changes everything here is **cyanuric acid**. CYA protects
chlorine from sunlight and simultaneously holds it back from working, so the
higher your CYA, the more free chlorine you need for the same effect. That's why
CDC raises the home minimum from 1 ppm to 2 ppm the moment CYA is present, and
why "I added chlorine and nothing happened" is usually a CYA story rather than a
chlorine story.

<UncommonTip>
  **A test that reads only total chlorine can show a healthy number on a pool
  with no working sanitizer.**

  Plenty of inexpensive strips report total chlorine and don't separate free
  from combined. Read one on a pool sitting at 0.2 ppm free and 2.8 ppm
  combined and it returns roughly 3 ppm — a number that looks completely fine.
  Meanwhile nothing in that pool is being sanitized, and algae is already
  winning.

  This is the mechanism behind the most common complaint in pool care: *my
  strips say the chlorine is fine but the water went green.* The strips weren't
  lying. They were answering a different question.

  Before you trust any reading, find out which number your test actually
  measures. If the packaging says "total chlorine" or just "chlorine" with a
  single pad, it cannot tell you whether your pool is sanitized. A test that
  reports free and total separately gives you combined for free, by subtraction
  — and that difference is the most useful number on the whole panel.
</UncommonTip>

## How to raise free chlorine

Add chlorine — but choose the form deliberately, because each one brings
something else with it.

- **Liquid chlorine (sodium hypochlorite)** adds chlorine and salt. Nothing
  else. It's the right choice while you're correcting a problem.
- **Cal-hypo** adds chlorine and calcium hardness. Fine unless your calcium is
  already high.
- **Dichlor and trichlor** add chlorine *and cyanuric acid* — roughly 0.9 ppm
  CYA per ppm of chlorine for dichlor, roughly 0.6 for trichlor. Convenient, and
  the reason so many pools drift into a CYA problem over a season.

Doses depend entirely on your volume, so there's no useful "add this much" here.
Run the numbers in the [chlorine dosage
calculator](/tools/chlorine-dosage-calculator), and if you don't know your
gallons, start with the [volume calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) —
every figure downstream is wrong if that one is.

<SafetyWarning>
  Never mix pool chemicals. Add each product to the water separately — never to
  another chemical, never into a bucket that held one, and never combine two
  types of shock. Chlorine and acid together produce chlorine gas. Always add
  chemicals to water, never water to chemicals, and don't swim until free
  chlorine is back in range.
</SafetyWarning>

## How to lower combined chlorine

You don't lower it directly. You destroy it with more chlorine.

Combined chlorine breaks down when free chlorine is raised high enough for long
enough — that's what shocking is actually for. Not "adding chlorine because the
pool looks off," but oxidizing accumulated chloramines until the combined
reading drops back toward zero.

Which means the instinct most people have is exactly backwards. A pool that
smells strongly of chlorine and stings your eyes doesn't have too much chlorine.
It has too little free chlorine and too much combined, and the fix is more
chlorine, not less.

## Order of operations

Chemistry adjustments interact, so sequence matters more than most guides admit.

1. **Circulation first.** Nothing you add works in water that isn't moving.
2. **Total alkalinity next.** TA buffers pH. Correct it first or pH won't hold
   still while you chase it.
3. **pH after that.** Above 7.8, chlorine's effectiveness falls off sharply, so
   correcting pH makes every chlorine dose work harder.
4. **Then chlorine.** Test, dose, wait a full turnover, retest.
5. **CYA last, and rarely.** It only goes up with stabilized products and only
   comes down by replacing water.

Wait a full turnover between adjustments and retest before adding anything else.
Most over-correction happens because someone tested twenty minutes after dosing.

## What goes wrong when it's off

**Free chlorine too low:** algae within days in warm water, and no protection
against what swimmers bring in. See [green pool
water](/problems/green-pool-water).

**Free chlorine very high:** not the hazard people assume at ordinary pool
levels, but it will bleach swimsuits and some liners, and it makes pH readings
unreliable on some tests. It also falls on its own — the usual answer is to stop
adding and wait.

**Combined chlorine elevated:** the smell, the eye sting, and a pool that feels
harsh. It's a signal that free chlorine has been too low relative to the load.

**Total chlorine watched instead of free:** the failure mode in the tip above —
a normal-looking number on an unsanitized pool.

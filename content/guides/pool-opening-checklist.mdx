Most pool opening checklists are a list of tasks. The tasks aren't the hard
part — the sequence is. Do chemistry before circulation and you'll spend a week
correcting numbers in water that was never moving, then wonder why nothing held.

Six steps, in this order.

## 1. Take the cover off without emptying it into the pool

Pump the standing water off the cover first, then sweep the debris, then remove
it. The water sitting on a winter cover is the worst water on the property, and
every gallon of it that goes into the pool is chlorine demand you'll pay for
later.

Even a careful removal drops some in. That's normal. What isn't normal is
dragging a full cover across the pool because it seemed faster.

Once it's off, clean and dry it properly before storage. A cover put away damp
comes out next autumn with mildew that transfers straight back into the pool.

## 2. Reconnect and inspect the equipment before you power anything up

Reinstall drain plugs, sight glasses and the pressure gauge. Check the pump lid
o-ring while you have the lid off — it's spent the winter compressed and dry,
and it's the most common reason a pool won't prime on opening day.

Look at every union and valve for freeze damage. Cracked plastic often doesn't
show until there's pressure behind it, so this is your one easy chance to spot
it dry.

## 3. Get water moving before anything else

Fill to mid-skimmer, prime the pump and start circulating. Let it run.

*Why this comes third and not sixth:* every chemical you add works on the water
it reaches. In a static pool, a dose sits in a layer near where you poured it,
reads wrong on a test taken elsewhere, and gets attributed to the product not
working. Circulation is what turns a dose into a treatment.

If the pump won't catch, stop and fix that before continuing — see [pool pump
not priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming). Nothing below this line works
without it.

## 4. Clean the filter, then brush everything

Backwash a sand or DE filter, or pull and hose the cartridge. A filter that
finished last season loaded starts this one loaded.

Then brush the walls, the floor, the steps and the waterline — including behind
the ladder and into the step corners, which is where spring algae starts and
where most people don't reach.

## 5. Test, then correct in the right order

Now — and only now — get a full set of readings.

<ProductBlock id="drop-test-kit" />

Correct in this sequence, because each step moves the next:

1. **Total alkalinity** first. It buffers pH; correct it and pH stops fighting
   you.
2. **pH** next, into the 7.0–7.8 range CDC gives for home pools. Above 7.8,
   chlorine works noticeably less well, so this step makes step three cheaper.
3. **Free chlorine** last. CDC's home-pool minimum is 1 ppm, or 2 ppm where
   cyanuric acid is present — and treat those as a floor rather than a target.

Every dose depends on your actual volume. Run it through the [chlorine dosage
calculator](/tools/chlorine-dosage-calculator), and if you've never measured,
the [volume calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) takes two minutes and
makes every number after it correct.

Wait a full turnover between adjustments and retest before adding more. Most
opening-week over-correction comes from testing twenty minutes after dosing.

<SafetyWarning>
  Never mix pool chemicals. Add each product to the water separately — never to
  another chemical, never into a bucket that held one, and never combine two
  types of shock. Chlorine and acid together produce chlorine gas. Always add
  chemicals to water, never water to chemicals, and keep your face out of the
  vapor when pouring acid.
</SafetyWarning>

## 6. Shock, then leave it alone

With circulation running and the filter clean, shock the pool and let it work.
Retest after a full turnover rather than the same evening.

If the water clouds after shocking, that's usually dead organics the filter
hasn't caught yet, and it clears with run time — see [cloudy pool
water](/problems/cloudy-pool-water) if it doesn't.

<UncommonTip>
  **Open when the water is still cold, not when you want to swim.**

  Algae growth slows dramatically as water temperature drops, and below roughly
  60°F it's close to dormant. A pool opened at that point is a pool you clean
  rather than a pool you rescue — the winter's debris is sitting there inert,
  waiting to be filtered out, and it hasn't turned into anything yet.

  Wait for warm weather and you're not opening a dirty pool. You're opening a
  pool that has been quietly incubating for weeks under a cover, in still water,
  with no sanitizer. That's a bloom in progress, and clearing it costs several
  days and a meaningful amount of chemical.

  The cost difference is the point. Opening early costs you a few weeks of
  running the pump before anyone swims. Opening late costs chemicals, filter
  cleanings, and the weekend nobody could use the pool. The cheap version is the
  one that feels premature.
</UncommonTip>

## What makes opening harder than it needs to be

Nearly all of it is decided the previous autumn. A pool closed with balanced
water and a clean filter opens clear. A pool closed dirty opens green, every
time, and no amount of opening-day effort fully makes that up.

The other repeat offender is leaves left on the cover through winter. They break
down, stain the cover, and the tannins go into the pool the moment it comes off.

## When to stop and call someone

- **Cracked plumbing or a split pump housing** from freeze damage. Pressure
  testing and repair, not a DIY fix.
- **The heater won't fire** after winter. Gas work is licensed work in most
  states — stop at the safe checks.
- **The pump won't prime** after you've checked the lid o-ring, water level and
  valves.
- **Water level dropped over winter** with no obvious cause. That's a possible
  leak, and finding it needs equipment.
- **The shell or deck has moved** — new cracks, a lifted coping stone, tile
  that's come away. Structural, and it gets worse.

Sending you to a technician earns us nothing compared with selling you another
jug of shock. Freeze damage is one of the places where that's clearly the right
call anyway.

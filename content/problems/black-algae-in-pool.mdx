Almost every page about a black algae pool skips the only question that matters
first. It assumes the spots are algae, then hands you one protocol: stainless
brush, chlorine held at shock level, repeat for weeks.

That protocol is right on plaster. On a fiberglass pool it is both useless and
damaging — because on fiberglass, black spots usually are not alive at all.

So the first question is not how to kill it. It is what your pool is made of,
and what happens when you brush the spot.

## What you're actually looking at

Find your surface first. It narrows the diagnosis further than any test kit
will.

### Plaster, gunite, or pebblecrete

**Black algae.** Dark blue-green to charcoal rather than truly black. Small —
roughly pencil-eraser sized — raised, and gritty under a fingertip. Always
attached, never drifting. It favors shaded corners, steps, the area behind
ladders, the waterline, grout lines, and any rough patch where the plaster has
degraded.

*The test:* it resists a fingernail and resists a brush. It does not smear and
it does not powder.

**Mineral stain.** Flat, dull, and it will not dislodge at all no matter how
hard you brush. Usually rust, brown, or purple-black rather than charcoal, and
often in a drip or streak shape rather than a spot.

### Fiberglass

This is where the standard advice does damage.

**Cobalt spotting.** Hard, gray-black marks, sometimes with small bumps. The
giveaway is depth: it sits *beneath* the surface, embedded in the gelcoat rather
than growing on it. Water penetrates the gelcoat by osmosis and reacts with
cobalt in the polyester resin underneath. It is a substrate failure. No chlorine
level touches it, because there is nothing living to kill — the repair is
gelcoat work.

*The test:* brush it and it powders into the water immediately, then comes back
in exactly the same place within days. **That "powders and returns" pattern is
the single most reliable tell that you are not dealing with algae.**

**Hydraulic blistering.** Dark circular marks, sometimes with small bubbles
under the finish, occasionally rupturing into shallow craters. That is moisture
pressure behind the coating — again a substrate problem, not a water chemistry
one.

### Vinyl

**Black algae is possible but uncommon.** A liner gives it very little to grip.

Dark spots that smear or lift when you brush firmly are usually mold or trapped
debris rather than algae. And spots that are *under* the liner are a different
problem entirely — no pool chemical reaches them, and scrubbing at the liner
from inside will not help.

### The brush test, in one line each

- **Resists the brush** — black algae.
- **Powders away, returns in the same spot** — cobalt through a gelcoat.
- **Smears or lifts off** — mold or debris.
- **Will not move at all** — mineral stain. [Identify it by color and two
  field tests](/problems/pool-stains-identification).

Cobalt, manganese staining, and hydraulic blistering each have their own
treatment, and none of them is chemistry you pour in the water. This article
takes you as far as knowing which one you have; we are writing the treatment for
each separately.

## Why black algae is so hard to kill

Worth understanding, because every step of the treatment below exists for one of
these reasons.

**It is cyanobacteria, not a true algae.** That single fact explains why
algaecides formulated against green algae underperform on it, and why it shrugs
off chlorine levels that would erase a green bloom overnight.

**It builds a multilayer defense.** There is a tough polysaccharide and protein
outer shell — often called the helmet — with a biofilm of proteins, lipids and
carotenoids beneath it. Both block sanitizer from reaching the living cells
inside. Chlorine simply cannot get through an intact one, which is why pouring
in more of it changes nothing.

**Its holdfasts root into the surface.** What you can see is the head of the
colony. The anchor sits below the plaster line, inside the pores. Remove the
visible part and the plant is still there — which is exactly why it returns in
the same spots rather than new ones.

**It photosynthesizes.** A sunlit shallow end with degraded plaster is close to
ideal habitat for it.

Compare that with an ordinary [green pool](/problems/green-pool-water), where
the algae is suspended in the water, has no shell, and dies as soon as you get
chlorine to it. Same word, completely different fight.

And if the spots are yellow-brown rather than charcoal and wipe into a plume
when brushed, that is [mustard algae](/problems/mustard-algae-in-pool) — the
other stubborn one, with a biofilm instead of a shell and no holdfasts.

## Test these numbers first

None of these identifies the spots. The surface material and the brush test
already did that. What they decide is whether the treatment can work at all.

| Test | Target | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| Cyanuric acid | Know the number before dosing | CYA decides what "shock level" actually means in your pool. A flat ppm target is meaningless without it |
| pH | 7.2–7.6 | Above 7.8, chlorine effectiveness falls sharply — you would be running the whole protocol at reduced strength |
| Free chlorine | ≥ 1 ppm, or ≥ 2 ppm with CYA present, as a baseline | Confirms where you are starting from |

CDC's home-pool guidance puts free chlorine at a minimum of 1 ppm, or at least
2 ppm where cyanuric acid is in use, with pH from 7.0 to 7.8. Those are
maintenance figures, not treatment figures — the level you hold against black
algae is far higher, and it scales with your CYA. [Chlorine
basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) covers why, and the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) turns it into an actual dose.

## The fix, cheapest first — plaster and gunite only

**If your pool is fiberglass or vinyl and the spots powder and return, skip this
section entirely.** Nothing in it applies, and step 1 will damage your surface.

### 1. Brush every spot, hard, with a stainless steel brush. Free if you own one.

*Why this works:* you are not cleaning. You are breaching the protective shell
so that chlorine can reach the cells underneath. Until that happens, nothing
else you do works — this is the step the whole treatment stands on.

**Stainless is for plaster and gunite only.** On vinyl or fiberglass it causes
permanent damage, and on those surfaces nylon is the only correct choice.

### 2. Hold chlorine high, and brush before every dose. Under $30.

*Why this works:* the shell rebuilds. That is the part most people miss — they
brush once at the start, then dose for a week against a colony that resealed
itself on day two. Brushing has to happen immediately before each dose, not once
at the beginning.

The level you hold is relative to your CYA, not a fixed number, which is why the
flat "20–30 ppm" figure repeated everywhere is wrong for a lot of pools in both
directions. Run your own numbers through the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) and read [chlorine
basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) for the CYA relationship.

<ProductBlock id="liquid-chlorine" />

### 3. Rub a trichlor tablet directly on each spot. Under $30.

Gloved, one spot at a time, straight onto the brushed colony.

*Why this works:* it puts concentrated chlorine on the exposed colony instead of
diluting the same chlorine across thousands of gallons. For a handful of
stubborn spots this does more than another gallon in the skimmer.

<SafetyWarning>
  Wear chemical-resistant gloves. Never handle a trichlor tablet with bare
  hands. Never use this method on a vinyl liner — it will bleach the spot
  permanently. Trichlor is acidic and must never be mixed with or stored beside
  cal-hypo; the combination can ignite. Never mix pool chemicals, and add each
  one to the water separately.
</SafetyWarning>

<ProductBlock id="trichlor-tablets" />

### 4. Clean the filter and check the equipment. Under $30.

*Why this works:* colonies live in the filter media and the plumbing as well as
on the wall. Treat only what you can see and you leave a reservoir that re-seeds
the pool as soon as you stop. Clean or replace the media, and run any automatic
cleaner or brush head through a chlorine soak before it goes back in.

### 5. Keep going after the spots disappear. Weeks, not days.

Be realistic about the timeline. Visible spots typically clear in one to two
weeks. Full eradication takes considerably longer, and **stopping when the pool
looks clean is the single most common reason black algae returns.**

### What does not work

**Draining and refilling.** It survives in the filter, the plumbing, and the
pores of the surface — none of which leave with the water. You will refill and
watch it come back, having paid for the water and taken on the structural risk
of an empty shell for nothing.

**A single shock treatment.** One dose against a sealed colony is one dose
wasted. Without brushing, the shell holds.

**Algaecide on an unbrushed colony.** Same problem, more expensive. And products
aimed at green algae are the wrong chemistry for cyanobacteria in the first
place.

## Why it comes back

**Same spots every time means the holdfasts survived.** That is under-treatment,
not reinfection. It is also the most useful diagnostic you have: new spots in
new places is a circulation or sanitation problem, while the same spots
reappearing is the original colony you did not finish.

**A degraded surface gives it grip.** This is why an old, rough, or etched
plaster finish reinfects far faster than a new one — and why, on a pool that has
fought the same colony for years, resurfacing is sometimes the actual fix rather
than another season of chemicals.

**Shaded dead zones and poor circulation.** Corners the returns never reach are
where it establishes first.

**A filter or cleaner that was never sanitized.** You can treat the pool
perfectly and reintroduce the colony from your own equipment.

<UncommonTip>
  **Before you test anything, ask what the pool is made of.**

  Cyanobacteria needs microscopic surface texture to anchor its holdfasts.
  Plaster, gunite and pebblecrete give it exactly that. A smooth gelcoat or a
  vinyl liner does not.

  So black spots on a fiberglass pool are usually not algae at all. They are
  cobalt coming through the gelcoat from underneath — which is why they powder
  away under a brush and reappear in precisely the same place within days, a
  behavior no living colony has.

  The cost of missing this is not small. Fiberglass pool owners routinely spend
  months holding chlorine at 20–30 ppm against something that was never alive,
  and take a stainless brush to a gelcoat surface while doing it, adding
  permanent scratching to a problem that already needed professional repair.

  The surface material narrows the diagnosis before a single test strip comes
  out of the bottle. No page ranking for this query asks the question first, and
  it is the one that decides everything after it.
</UncommonTip>

## Is it safe to swim?

Worth being careful about this, because it is easy to find confident claims in
both directions.

Black algae is cyanobacteria, and some cyanobacteria produce cyanotoxins. But
most of that evidence concerns natural water bodies — lakes, ponds, reservoirs
during a bloom — not chlorinated residential pools, and those are different
environments.

[VERIFY: whether CDC or a comparable authority makes any specific statement
about cyanobacteria in chlorinated residential pools. Not found in research; do
not import the lake-bloom warning to fill the gap.]

So rather than import the lake warning, here is what can be said plainly: black
algae established in your pool means sanitation has failed somewhere. Whatever
else is in the water has not been controlled either, and that is the real
argument for staying out. Check free chlorine against the CDC figures above,
treat the pool, and do not let the size of the spots talk you into thinking it
is cosmetic.

## When to stop and call someone

- **A fiberglass pool with spots that powder and return.** That is a gelcoat
  problem and it needs a resurfacing assessment, not chemistry. Every week spent
  dosing is a week of the underlying failure getting worse.
- **Blistering, bubbling, or small craters in the surface.** Moisture behind the
  coating. Not a water problem.
- **Black algae that survives a full, correctly executed treatment cycle** —
  brushed before every dose, chlorine held at the right CYA-relative level,
  filter cleaned. That usually means surface degradation deep enough that
  resurfacing is the real answer.
- **Spots under a vinyl liner.** Nothing you add to the water reaches them.
- **Recurrence in the same spots after three complete cycles.** Stop buying
  chemicals and get the surface assessed.

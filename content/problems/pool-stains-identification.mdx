  - q: "How do you tell the difference between a pool stain and algae?"
    a: "Brush it. Algae is alive and sits on the surface — it dislodges, clouds the water, and grows back in the same spot within days. A stain is discoloration in the surface itself and will not move at all no matter how hard you brush. If brushing changes nothing, you have a stain and the two field tests will tell you which kind."
  - q: "Will shock remove pool stains?"
    a: "For organic stains, yes — chlorine oxidizes leaf, acorn and worm marks, and most lift within about a day.     a: "Usually, yes. Both organic and metal stains are treated in a full pool — that is the normal path, not a compromise. Draining is the higher-risk option, not the thorough one: an empty shell can float or crack, and the stain often is not in the plaster deep enough to justify it."

Every page about pool stains opens with a color chart. That is backwards.

Color narrows the possibilities, but it does not settle them — brown could be
organic or it could be iron, and gray could be four different things with four
different treatments. The two field tests settle it, they take five minutes, and
they cost about six dollars.

So run the tests first. Use color afterwards to work out which *kind* of organic
or which *kind* of metal you are looking at.

## The two field tests

Run both on the same patch of stain. Between them they place almost every stain
into the right family.

### Test 1 — the chlorine tablet

Hold a trichlor tablet against the stain for a few minutes. Use gloves, or wedge
it against the spot with a pole.

**If the stain lightens, it is organic.** Chlorine oxidizes organic material, so
a leaf mark or a worm stain fades visibly where the tablet sat.

<SafetyWarning>
  Plaster and pebble surfaces only. **Never do this on a vinyl liner** —
  concentrated chlorine held against vinyl bleaches the pattern out permanently,
  and you will trade a stain for a white patch that cannot be fixed. Wear
  chemical-resistant gloves; never hold a trichlor tablet in a bare hand.
  Trichlor is acidic and must never be mixed or stored with cal-hypo.
</SafetyWarning>

### Test 2 — the vitamin C tablet

Crush a plain vitamin C tablet into a sock or thin cloth and hold it against the
stain for thirty seconds to a few minutes.

**If the stain lightens, it is metal — most likely iron.** This one often works
fast enough to leave a conspicuously clean patch in the shape of the sock, which
is as unambiguous as pool diagnostics get.

One caveat that saves a wrong turn: **copper responds better to citric acid than
to ascorbic**. So a weak vitamin C result on a teal or blue-green stain does not
rule out metal. It may just be the wrong acid for that metal.

### What the results mean

| Result | Family | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Chlorine lightens it** | Organic | Leaves, acorns, worms, tannins, algae residue |
| **Vitamin C lightens it** | Metal | Iron most likely; copper may respond weakly |
| **Neither does anything** | Neither | Scale, cobalt, or something under the surface rather than in it |

That third row matters. If nothing touches it, stop buying stain products —
you are looking at a mineral deposit sitting *on* the surface, or a substrate
problem *below* it, and neither is a staining problem at all.

## Color as a shortlist, not an answer

With the family established, color tells you which specific culprit to chase.

**Green, brown, or yellow, often in drifts where debris collects** — organic.
Leaves, acorns, worms, tannins. Extremely common at spring opening under a cover
that collected leaves all winter. Also common in the days after clearing a
[green pool](/problems/green-pool-water): dead algae settles, sits on the floor
while the filter catches up, and marks the plaster where it rested.

**Rust orange to brown** — iron. Well water, corroding pipework, or something
metal that sat on the floor. A hairpin or a can tab leaves a perfect little
signature.

**Teal, blue-green, or greenish-black** — copper. Do not stop at cleaning this
one; see the next section, because it usually has a cause still running.

**Purple** — ambiguous, and worth its own line. It can be manganese. It can also
be **copper cyanurate**: copper staining that turns purple when cyanuric acid is
high. If you have purple staining, test CYA as well as metals, because it is
pointing at two problems at once.

**Gray or black** — the least conclusive color on the chart. It could be
manganese, copper, black algae, or cobalt bleeding through a fiberglass gelcoat.
Those have nothing in common treatment-wise. [Black algae and its
lookalikes](/problems/black-algae-in-pool) covers the brush test that separates
living growth from staining, and the cobalt case on fiberglass.

**White or gray, crusty and rough to the touch** — not a stain at all. That is
calcium scale, a mineral deposit sitting on the surface rather than a
discoloration in it, which is exactly why neither field test does anything to
it. [Milky white pool water](/problems/milky-white-pool-water) covers the
saturation chemistry that produces it.

## Test these numbers first

| Test | What it tells you about *this* problem |
|---|---|
| pH | Low now, or low historically, points at copper coming out of your equipment |
| Total alkalinity | Low TA is what lets pH swing low in the first place |
| Calcium hardness | High, with crusty white deposits, means scale rather than stain |
| Cyanuric acid | High CYA plus purple staining points at copper cyanurate |
| Metals | Iron and copper in the water mean the stain has a live source still feeding it |

If you are working from a municipal water quality report, note that those give
**total** hardness while your test kit reads **calcium** hardness — roughly
70–80% of the total, with magnesium making up the rest. The calcium figure you
measure will come in below the number on the report.

## Where the metal actually came from

A stain is a symptom. **If the metal is still dissolved in the water, the stain
comes back after any treatment you run** — which is why this section matters
more than the treatment section below it.

**Well water.** The most common source, and it arrives on every fill and every
top-off, not just once.

**Copper algaecide.** Cheap copper-based formulas are the single most common
deliberate introduction. People add metal to their own pool to kill algae, then
treat the staining as a mystery.

**The heat exchanger.** Low pH strips copper out of a heater's internals and
carries it into the pool. This is the one almost nobody checks — see below.

**Corroding fittings and pipework**, and **ionizers**, which work by putting
metal in the water on purpose.

Two of those deserve a second look, because they are the ones that quietly keep
feeding the pool. **Well water is not a one-time event** — every top-off through
a hot summer adds more iron, so a pool that was treated successfully in May can
be stained again by August without anything having gone wrong. And **ionizers
work by design**, putting metal into the water deliberately; if you have one and
you have staining, those two facts are related.

The prevention that actually holds is unglamorous: test the fill water, not just
the pool; sequester on every top-off so metal never gets the chance to plate out;
and never let pH sit low for long. Dose sequestrant against your actual water
volume rather than a guess — the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) is the input every product label
assumes you already have.

## Treatment, cheapest first

Deliberately brief — this page is about identification, and each treatment below
has its own article with the full protocol.

**1. Organic — brush, chlorinate, remove the source.** Free to under $30. Brush
the stain, raise chlorine, and get rid of whatever was rotting there. Most
organic stains lift within about 24 hours. Use nylon on vinyl and fiberglass;
stainless is for plaster and gunite only.

<ProductBlock id="nylon-pool-brush" />

<ProductBlock id="stainless-algae-brush" />

**2. Metal, small and localized — ascorbic acid held on the spot.** Under $30.
The same chemistry as the field test, left in place longer.

**3. Metal, widespread — ascorbic acid broadcast, then a sequestrant
immediately.** Under $30 to under $150.

Two failure modes kill more of these treatments than anything else, and both are
avoidable:

**Chlorine destroys ascorbic acid.** Free chlorine above roughly 3 ppm breaks it
down before it can act on the stain. Bring FC down to about 1-2 ppm and switch
the chlorinator off before you dose. Run it at normal chlorine levels and
nothing happens — and the owner concludes the stain was never metal, which sends
them down the wrong branch for good. Be aware of the trade-off you are making:
the pool sits at low chlorine while this runs, which is an algae risk, so watch
it and have a plan to bring chlorine back up straight afterwards.
[Chlorine basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) covers doing that safely.

**Ascorbic acid removes the stain, not the metal.** The iron is still in your
water when the plaster looks clean. Without a sequestrant it re-plates and the
stain returns within weeks — the second most common reason these treatments
"fail" when they actually worked exactly as designed.

<ProductBlock id="ascorbic-acid" />

**4. Scale — not scrubbable.** Controlled acid, and usually a service job. No
brush removes it, and trying hard enough to matter will damage the surface.

**5. Acid washing — an escalation, not a step.** An acid wash removes a layer of
plaster in order to remove a stain that may not be in the plaster at all. It is
not reversible, and every pool surface has a finite number of them in it. Treat
it as a last resort after identification, never as a first response.

### What does not work

**Shocking a metal stain.** Oxidizing the water is what drove the metal onto the
surface to begin with. Shocking can darken it.

**Scrubbing scale.** It is a mineral deposit, not dirt.

**Generic "stain remover" bought before identification.** Half of these are
acids and half are oxidizers — the two opposite treatments. Buying before you
know the family gives you a coin flip on whether you improve the stain or set it.

<UncommonTip>
  **A teal stain is an autopsy on your heater.**

  Copper does not arrive in a pool on its own. So a teal or blue-green stain in
  a pool that has never seen a copper algaecide is not really a stain problem at
  all — and the most common source is the heater.

  Water held at low pH strips copper out of the heat exchanger and carries it
  into the pool, where it plates onto the plaster. The stain is evidence of the
  inside of your heater dissolving. And unless the pH has been corrected, it is
  still dissolving right now, while you shop for stain remover.

  This is why the usual sequence ends badly: the owner treats the plaster, the
  stain returns a month later, they treat it again, and nobody looks at the
  heater until it fails outright and needs replacing. The stain was the early
  warning, and it got cleaned off twice.

  If a teal stain appears and there is a heater on the system, **your pH history
  matters more than your stain remover.**
</UncommonTip>

## When to stop and call someone

- **Teal or blue-green staining, a heater on the system, and any history of low
  pH.** That is a corrosion assessment, not a cleaning job, and the stain is the
  cheapest warning you will get.
- **Stains that return within weeks of a correct treatment.** The metal is still
  in the water and the source has not been found. More treatment will not find
  it.
- **Hardened scale on the heater, the salt cell, or at the waterline.** Scale
  inside equipment is a [repair](/pool-repair) conversation, not a brush one.
- **Anyone considering an acid wash or a full drain.** Draining a pool risks
  hydrostatic pop-out — an empty shell can lift or crack — and it is rarely what
  a stain actually requires.
- **Staining on fiberglass that responds to nothing.** Likely cobalt coming
  through the gelcoat, which is a substrate failure. [Black algae and its
  lookalikes](/problems/black-algae-in-pool) explains how to recognize it.

Telling you to book a corrosion assessment instead of buying three bottles of
stain remover costs us the sale in this article. That is the trade, and the
heater is worth more than the referral.

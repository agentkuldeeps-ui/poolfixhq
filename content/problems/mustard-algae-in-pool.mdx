Three completely different things show up as yellow dust on a pool floor, and
their treatments have nothing in common. Pollen is a skimming and filtration
job. Sand is an equipment fault. Only one of the three is alive.

Most pages on this topic skip straight to the mustard algae protocol. If you
have pollen, that protocol costs you a week and a tank of chlorine and does
nothing, because there was never anything to kill.

So identify it first.

## What you're actually looking at

| | Where it sits | Brushed, what happens | Comes back? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mustard algae** | Walls, steps, ladders, light fixtures — shaded, low-flow spots | Clouds into a plume | **Yes — same spot, 1–2 days** |
| **Pollen** | Clumped and floating on the surface | Disperses, re-collects on top | Only if more blows in |
| **Filter sand or DE** | Flat layer on the floor, heaviest near the returns | Puffs up, settles back | Only if the filter fault remains |
| **Dead algae** | Even haze, follows a treatment | Disperses as fine dust | No |

### Mustard algae

Yellow, tan, or yellow-brown. It clings to walls, steps, ladders, light
fixtures and skimmer crevices, and it strongly prefers shade and dead zones —
the wall away from the sun, behind the ladder, the corner your returns never
quite reach.

**The definitive test is regrowth.** Brush a patch clean and watch it for two
days. Pollen is debris and does not grow back. Mustard algae is alive, and it
reattaches to the same spot.

**On texture, sources genuinely disagree, and it is worth saying so.** Some
describe mustard algae as slimy; others describe it as dry and dusty and
specifically *not* slimy the way green algae is. Both descriptions come from
people who deal with pools for a living. We are not going to pick one and
present it as fact — texture is a weak signal here, and if you are standing at
the edge of your pool trying to decide based on how it feels, you are using the
least reliable test available. Use the regrowth test instead. It settles the
question in two days and it does not depend on whose description of "slimy" you
happened to read.

### Pollen

Seasonal, and it arrives with tree pollen season — if there is a yellow film on
your car and the patio furniture, that is your answer. It tends to clump and
float rather than disperse evenly through the water, it has no preference for
shade, and it is fine enough to pass straight through some filters.

It does not come back after removal. More may blow in tomorrow, but what you
took out stays out.

### Sand or DE from the filter

Gritty and coarse if you rub it between your fingers — actually granular,
where algae and pollen are not. It settles in a flat layer, and the giveaway is
**location: heaviest near the return lines**, because that is where it came
into the pool.

It does not return after vacuuming unless the fault is still there. Recurring
grit near the returns after a proper vacuum is a filter problem, not a water
problem — a broken lateral or standpipe on a sand filter, or torn grids on a DE
filter. No amount of chlorine addresses it, and we will cover the repair
separately.

### Dead algae after a treatment

If this followed a green or yellow phase you already treated, fine tan dust
that reads as haze rather than a defined film is the corpse of the last bloom.
That is a filtration and vacuuming job. [Green pool
water](/problems/green-pool-water) covers the clearing phase.

## Why mustard algae survives chlorine that kills green algae

**The colony sits under a protective biofilm.** That single fact explains the
whole treatment.

The free chlorine you measure in the open water is not the concentration
reaching the cells beneath that film. So when your test reads perfectly normal
and the yellow is still there, **your test is not wrong — it is answering a
different question.** It is telling you about the water. It is not telling you
about the sheltered environment two millimeters under a coating on your wall.

That is the single most common way this problem gets misread. People re-test,
get a good number, conclude the kit is faulty or the algae is something exotic,
and start buying specialty products. The number was right all along.

Clearing it therefore takes two things together: the film broken mechanically,
and a chlorine level high enough, held long enough, to push through what
regrows. Either one alone fails.

It also prefers shade, which is not incidental. It establishes where UV is not
helping your chlorine and where circulation is weakest — the two conditions that
make its life easiest.

Compare [green algae](/problems/green-pool-water), which is suspended in the
water with no protective layer and dies as soon as chlorine reaches it. And
[black algae](/problems/black-algae-in-pool), which builds an even tougher shell
and roots into the surface itself. Mustard sits between the two.

## Test these numbers first

None of these identifies the yellow. What they decide is whether treatment can
work at all.

| Test | Why it matters here |
|---|---|
| Cyanuric acid | Decides what "shock level" actually means in your pool. A flat ppm target is meaningless without it |
| pH | Above 7.8, chlorine effectiveness drops sharply — you would run the whole protocol at reduced strength |
| Free chlorine | Your starting point, and the input to the overnight test below |

CDC's home-pool guidance puts free chlorine at a minimum of 1 ppm, or at least
2 ppm where cyanuric acid is in use, with pH between 7.0 and 7.8. Those are
maintenance figures — the level you hold against mustard algae is higher and
scales with your CYA. [Chlorine basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) covers the
relationship, and the [pool volume calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator)
turns it into an actual dose.

**The overnight chlorine loss test settles living versus not-living.** Measure
free chlorine at dusk, run the pump overnight, measure again at dawn before the
sun is on the water. A meaningful drop means something in your pool is consuming
chlorine. Pollen and sand consume nothing. This is the one number that
distinguishes a biological problem from a debris problem, and it costs you
nothing but two test strips and a night's wait.

## The fix, cheapest first

### 1. Brush every affected surface, hard. Free.

Behind the ladder, under the returns, the steps, around the light fixture, into
the skimmer throat. The shaded low-flow spots you would skip are exactly where
it lives.

*Why this works:* it breaks the biofilm so chlorine can reach the cells.
Brushing without shocking just relocates the colony — which is why "I brushed it
and it came back worse" is such a common report.

**Brush choice is decided by your surface, not your preference.** Stainless on
plaster and gunite only. On vinyl and fiberglass, nylon, always — stainless
causes permanent damage to both.

### 2. Raise chlorine and hold it there. Under $30.

*Why this works:* it is duration at level, not a single dose, that penetrates
what the colony rebuilds. Brush again before each addition rather than once at
the start.

Run your numbers through the [chlorine dosage
calculator](/tools/chlorine-dosage-calculator) and read [chlorine
basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) for the CYA relationship — the target is
relative to your stabilizer level, not a fixed figure.

Liquid chlorine is the cleaner choice for this job because it adds no calcium
and no CYA. Cal-hypo adds calcium every time you dose, and in a pool already
near saturation that trades an algae problem for a [milky white
pool](/problems/milky-white-pool-water).

<ProductBlock id="liquid-chlorine" />

### 3. Decontaminate everything that touched the water. Free.

*Why this works:* **this is the step that decides whether it returns.** Skip it
and you will do the whole protocol again in two weeks.

Swimsuits and towels: wash in detergent and dry fully in direct sunlight.
Floats, nets, brushes, vacuum hoses, the cleaner: soak in a diluted chlorine
solution. Anything that has been in another pool, a lake or the ocean gets
sanitized whether or not it looked dirty.

### 4. Clean the filter. Under $30.

*Why this works:* the filter is a reservoir. Treat the water, leave the filter
loaded, and the next run re-seeds the pool.

### 5. Algaecide — and be honest about what it does. Under $30.

*Why this works, and when it doesn't:* algaecide is a **preventative for after
the bloom is dead**, not the primary kill. Buying algaecide instead of doing
steps 1 through 4 is the most common way people spend money on this problem and
stay infected.

One caveat worth the money it saves: copper-based algaecides can stain plaster.
If your water already carries metals, a copper product trades an algae problem
for a staining problem.

## Why it comes back

Mustard algae survives out of the water, on fabric and equipment. A pool that
reinfects within days of a clean treatment is almost always being re-seeded by
its owner.

**The pool cleaner is the biggest offender.** Running a robotic or suction
cleaner during an active bloom drags spores across every surface it touches and
packs them into the filter — you are using the machine that cleans your pool to
distribute the infection through it. Take it out until treatment is finished,
then sanitize it before it goes back in.

**Swimsuits and towels** that were not washed and sun-dried put it straight back
on the next swim.

**Brushes, nets and vacuum hoses** moved between pools, or borrowed, carry it in.

**Dead zones with poor circulation** are where it establishes first, which is
why it reappears in the same corner rather than at random.

**Shade.** You are not going to move the tree, but it explains why the same wall
is always first and why that wall needs the most brushing.

<UncommonTip>
  **Pollen is waterproof. Mustard algae is not. That tells you which you have
  before you touch a brush.**

  Pollen grains carry a waxy, water-repellent outer coat — it is how they shed
  moisture out in the world. In a pool, that property means pollen clumps
  together and rides on the surface instead of dispersing through the water
  column. It is also why a skimmer lifts pollen out easily while a filter
  struggles with it once it finally sinks.

  Mustard algae does the exact opposite. Disturb it and it disperses into the
  water as a plume, because it has no reason to repel water — it lives in it.

  So look at where the yellow is sitting before you do anything else. Riding on
  the surface in clumps and rafts is pollen. Sitting on the walls and floor in
  shaded patches is not. That is a free identification from the pool deck, and
  it is available to you the moment you walk outside.
</UncommonTip>

## Is it safe to swim?

Worth being careful here rather than repeating a scare.

Mustard algae itself is not the hazard people often assume. What matters is what
its presence tells you: sanitation has failed somewhere, and a pool that cannot
hold chlorine against algae is not controlling anything else either. That is the
real argument for staying out until it is handled.

## When to stop and call someone

- **Yellow returns within days after three complete treatment cycles**,
  including full gear decontamination. At that point something is reintroducing
  it that the protocol is not reaching.
- **Recurring grit near the returns after vacuuming.** That is a filter fault —
  a broken lateral, standpipe, or torn DE grids — and it needs the filter opened,
  not more chemicals.
- **Chlorine that will not hold at the level needed no matter the dose.** That is
  a separate problem underneath this one, and it has to be solved first.
- **Any thought of draining.** Draining does not fix this: the algae survives in
  the filter, in the plumbing, and on your gear, all of which are still there
  when you refill. You would pay for the water, take on the structural risk of an
  empty shell, and reinfect the new fill from the same equipment.

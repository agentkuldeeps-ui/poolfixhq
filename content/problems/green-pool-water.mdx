Green pool water is algae, and algae is a symptom rather than the disease. The
disease is that free chlorine hit zero and stayed there. Spores are in every
pool all the time — they blow in, they come off swimsuits, they're in the rain.
What changed isn't that algae arrived. It's that nothing was killing it.

That distinction decides everything below. Treat the bloom and it comes back in
a two weeks. Fix why the chlorine went to zero and it doesn't.

## What you're actually looking at

Three things get called "green pool water" and only one of them is algae. Get
this wrong and you'll spend a week and a hundred dollars treating the wrong
problem.

**Algae** clouds the water column. You lose sight of the main drain, the walls
feel slippery, and the color is in the water rather than on it. It gets worse
overnight, not better, and it gets worse fastest in warm weather.

**Pollen** sits on the surface as a yellow-green film and the water underneath
stays clear. If you can still read the depth marker on the floor, it's pollen.
It arrives in a two-week window in spring, it collects in corners and along the
skimmer's throat, and a skimmer sock catches most of it. Chlorine does nothing
to it because there's nothing to kill.

**Dissolved metal** — usually copper — turns water green while leaving it
perfectly clear. The tell is timing: metal-green appears *after* you shock,
because oxidizing the water is what drops the metal out of solution. If your
pool went green within an hour of adding chlorine, you don't have algae. You
have copper from well water, from an ionizer, or from a copper-based algaecide
someone added earlier. Adding more chlorine makes that worse.

There's no clean way to separate a light algae bloom from heavy pollen at a
glance in a pool that's been uncovered for a week. Drop a white bucket of the
water and let it settle overnight. Sediment on the bottom is algae; a film on
top is pollen.

## What causes it

Free chlorine is consumed constantly — by sunlight, by swimmers, by leaves,
by the algae already in the water. When consumption outruns what you're adding,
free chlorine reaches zero. From that point, algae doubles roughly every day in
warm water. A pool that looked fine on Friday is properly green by Monday, and
that speed is why this feels like it happened overnight.

The three ways it usually gets to zero:

**Run time too short.** Chlorine that isn't circulated isn't reaching most of
the pool. A pump running four hours a day in July isn't turning the water over.

**Cyanuric acid too high.** CYA protects chlorine from sunlight, which is why
it's in every stabilized tablet. But it also holds chlorine back, and the higher
CYA climbs the more free chlorine you need for the same killing power. Pools run
on trichlor tabs all season creep up quietly, because every tablet adds CYA that
only leaves with water. This is the single most common reason a pool goes green
while the owner insists they've been adding chlorine — and they have been. It
just isn't working at that CYA level.

**Nobody tested for a two weeks.** Which is most cases, and there's no shame in
it.

## Test these numbers first

Before you add anything, get four readings. Guessing here is what turns a
three-day fix into a two-week one.

| Test | What you want | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| Free chlorine | ≥ 1 ppm, or ≥ 2 ppm with CYA present | CDC's home-pool figure. Zero confirms algae |
| pH | 7.0–7.8 | Above 7.8, chlorine gets sharply less effective |
| Cyanuric acid | The number that sets your chlorine target | High CYA is why shocking "doesn't work" |
| Total alkalinity | 80–120 ppm | Keeps pH from swinging while you dose |

One thing worth knowing, because most pool sites get it wrong: **CDC publishes
different numbers for home pools and public facilities.** The widely repeated
7.2–7.8 pH range is the public-facility figure. For home pools CDC gives
7.0–7.8, and at least 1 ppm free chlorine rather than the public 2 ppm — with at
least 2 ppm when cyanuric acid is present. Sites copy the commercial numbers at
homeowners without noticing. Use the home figures.


## The fix, cheapest first

### 1. Brush and run the pump. Free.

Brush the walls, the steps, and behind the ladder before you add anything, then
leave the pump running 24 hours a day until the pool clears.

*Why this works:* algae on a surface is physically shielded — chlorine reaches
the outer layer and stops. Brushing puts it into the water column where chlorine
can reach all of it, and continuous circulation is what carries the chlorine
there and the dead cells to the filter. Do this and everything you add next
works several times harder. Skip it and you'll buy more chemicals than you need.

<ProductBlock id="stainless-algae-brush" />

### 2. Liquid chlorine, held up for days. Under $30.

Raise free chlorine and keep it raised. Test twice a day and re-dose whenever it
falls. This is one purchase and one habit, not a product.

*Why this works:* a single dose is consumed within hours by the organic load
it's oxidizing, and the pool returns to zero overnight — which is exactly why
"I shocked it and it's still green" is the most common follow-up question on
this topic. Sustained chlorine is what kills a bloom. A brief spike isn't.

Use liquid chlorine rather than tablets while you're clearing. Tablets add
cyanuric acid with every one, and if high CYA is what caused this, more tablets
make the pool harder to clear. Our [chlorine dosage
calculator](/tools/chlorine-dosage-calculator) gives you the amount for your
actual volume — and if you don't know your gallons, start with the [volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator), because every dose on this page
depends on that number.

<SafetyWarning>
  Never mix pool chemicals. Add each one to the water separately — never to
  another chemical, never into a bucket that held one, and never down the
  skimmer while something else is dissolving. Chlorine and acid together
  produce chlorine gas. Always add chemicals to water, never water to
  chemicals, and don't swim until free chlorine is back in range.
</SafetyWarning>

### 3. Filter maintenance, and flocculant only if it's bad. Under $150.

If the filter can't move water, nothing above works. Watch the pressure gauge:
a rise of 8–10 psi over your clean starting pressure means backwash or clean the
cartridge — and during a bloom you may hit that several times a day. Sand that's
years old, or a cartridge that won't come clean, is worth replacing now rather
than fighting it.

*Why this works:* chlorine kills the algae; the filter is what actually removes
it. A dead bloom in a pool with a blocked filter stays cloudy indefinitely.

For a heavy bloom, flocculant drops the debris to the floor so you can vacuum
it to waste rather than through the filter. It works, but it costs you a large
volume of water and a slow manual vacuum — for most green pools, brushing and
filtration get there for less money and less hassle. Reach for it when the pool
is opaque, not when it's tinted.

<UncommonTip>
  **A clearing pool turns gray before it turns clear, and gray is the win.**
  Dead algae doesn't vanish — it loses its color and becomes fine gray or
  bluish white particulate suspended in the water, which usually looks *worse*
  than the green did. This is the exact point where people decide the treatment
  failed, stop dosing, and let the pool go back to green with a day of work
  left to do.

  Gray means the chlorine won and the job is now the filter's. Keep the pump
  running, keep backwashing as pressure climbs, and hold your chlorine — don't
  add anything new. If the water is gray rather than green, you're closer than
  you think.
</UncommonTip>

## How long it takes to clear

Honest ranges, assuming you're holding chlorine and running the pump around the
clock. Anything faster than this is a sales pitch.

- **Light green, still see the floor** — 2 to 3 days. Most of that is filtering.
- **Deep green, floor invisible** — 5 to 7 days, with the filter cleaned
  repeatedly.
- **Dark or swamp green, surface debris** — 1 to 2 weeks, and expect to clean
  or replace filter media.

The color goes long before the cloudiness does. Budget most of the time for
the gray phase, not the green one.

## What makes it come back

Clearing a bloom without fixing the cause buys you about two weeks.

Check cyanuric acid, and stop using stabilized tablets as your only sanitizer if
it's climbing. Lengthen pump run time through summer. Brush weekly — the spots
people miss are behind the ladder, under the skimmer throat, and the step
corners, and that's where the next bloom starts. Test twice a week rather than
when the water looks off, because by the time it looks off you're already at
zero.

If the water clouds again without turning green, that's a different problem —
see [cloudy pool water](/problems/cloudy-pool-water). For what free chlorine
actually is and why the number on your strip may not mean what you think, start
with [chlorine basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics).

## When to stop and call someone

Specific triggers, not a blanket disclaimer:

- **The pool won't hold chlorine at all** after several days of correct dosing.
  That points at very high CYA or a heavy organic load, and the answer may be
  partial drainage — worth a second opinion before you dump water.
- **The pump won't prime or the filter pressure won't rise.** Nothing on this
  page works without circulation. Start at [pool pump not
  priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming), and get someone in if that doesn't
  resolve it.
- **The water level drops while you're treating.** You may be refilling away
  your chemistry through a leak. Leak detection needs pressure-testing
  equipment, which is a service call.
- **Green returns within days of clearing, repeatedly.** Something structural is
  feeding it, and diagnosing that needs eyes on the pool.
- **Any smell of gas near the equipment pad, or a breaker that trips when the
  pump starts.** Stop. That's licensed work.

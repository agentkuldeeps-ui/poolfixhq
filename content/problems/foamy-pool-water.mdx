## Start here: which one do you have?

Before anything else, put a hand in it. This single test splits the problem into
two unrelated jobs, and almost every wasted afternoon on this topic comes from
skipping it.

| | **Foam** | **Bubbles** |
|---|---|---|
| **Touch test** | Dense, whitish, **does not pop** | Pops immediately |
| **How long it lasts** | Persists, often for days | Seconds |
| **What it is** | Organic material | Air |
| **Where it collects** | Across the surface, worst in corners | Streaming from the returns |
| **The job** | Water chemistry | Plumbing or equipment |

**Foam is not made of air.** That is the whole reason the touch test works — you
are feeling organic material that has lowered the water's surface tension enough
for the surface to hold together instead of breaking. Air bubbles have no such
scaffolding, so they collapse the moment you touch them.

If it pops, stop reading here. You have air getting in, and the usual causes are
a water level low enough that the skimmer is drawing air — anything under about
half-submerged will do it — or a leak on the suction side of the pump. Neither is
a chemical problem. [Pump priming and suction-side
air](/equipment/pump-not-priming) covers finding it.

Everything below is about foam.

## Rule out the ordinary explanation first

Most foam has a boring cause, and checking it costs nothing.

The same organic load deposits on the wall as a [scum ring at the
waterline](/problems/waterline-scum-ring) — foam in the water, ring on the
tile, one cause.

**Was the pool busy yesterday?** Sweat, sunscreen, lotions, hair product, and
detergent still in swimsuits are all organic. A heavy afternoon puts in enough to
foam by the next morning. This is the most common cause of foam by a wide margin,
it needs oxidation rather than sanitation, and it does not come back unless the
load repeats.

**Did you add algaecide recently?** Quaternary ammonium algaecides — "quats" —
foam, and overdosing any algaecide can. This foam usually clears on its own.

If neither applies, or if the foam keeps returning after you have dealt with
both, then it is worth going further. That is where the interesting answer is,
and it is in the section after next.

## What causes it, in order

| Cause | Tell | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Organic load** | Followed heavy use; foam is worst after a busy day | Oxidize — non-chlorine shock is enough |
| **Quat algaecide** | Followed an algaecide dose; chlorine also dropping | Wait it out, skim, switch products |
| **Low calcium hardness** | Foam keeps returning after the organics are handled | Test CH — see below |
| **Early algae** | Foam plus rising chlorine demand and falling FC | Treat the algae, check phosphates |
| **Air in the plumbing** | It pops — so it was never foam | Plumbing, not chemistry |

Two of those deserve more than a table row.

### Early algae is the one that surprises people

As chlorine kills off an early bloom, the dying algae releases compounds that
lower the water's surface tension — which is precisely the condition foam needs
to form. So foam can be the first visible sign of an algae problem you have not
seen yet, appearing before the water goes green.

The corroborating signal is chlorine demand. If foam shows up *and* free chlorine
is falling faster than usual, you are probably watching a bloom die rather than a
sunscreen problem. Check phosphates while you are there. Note that this mechanism
comes from a single trade source rather than published research — it is
chemically coherent and worth acting on, but we would rather tell you where it
comes from than present it as settled science. [Green pool
water](/problems/green-pool-water) covers the bloom itself.

### The sources disagree about phosphates

Worth flagging, because it changes what you would buy first. One of the two trade
sources behind this article opens with phosphates as the normal cause of foam.
The other treats organics as the most prevalent cause by some distance, with
phosphates appearing only as a secondary check once early algae is suspected.

We have written it the second way. If you lead with phosphates you send someone
to buy a phosphate remover for what is usually sunscreen and sweat. Test for
phosphates when foam comes with falling chlorine — not as the opening move.

## Test these numbers

The foam already told you it is organic. These tell you whether it will keep
happening.

| Test | Why it matters here |
|---|---|
| **Calcium hardness** | The one that decides whether foam persists. Below ~150 ppm it forms easily and stays |
| **Free chlorine** | Falling FC alongside foam points at early algae rather than bather load |
| **pH and total alkalinity** | Out-of-range water lets organics accumulate instead of being oxidized |

CDC's home-pool guidance puts free chlorine at a minimum of 1 ppm, or at least
2 ppm where cyanuric acid is in use, with pH between 7.0 and 7.8. The trade
sources here give tighter working ranges — total alkalinity 80–120 ppm, pH
7.4–7.6, calcium hardness 200–400 ppm — which are reasonable operating targets
but are not the same thing as public health guidance. We have kept the two
separate on purpose.

## What to do next, by cause

**If it followed heavy use — oxidize it.** Non-chlorine shock is the better tool
here, because the job is breaking down organics rather than killing anything.
It adds no chlorine, no calcium and no cyanuric acid, which matters if any of
those are already high.

<ProductBlock id="non-chlorine-shock" />

**If it followed an algaecide dose — wait, and skim.** It clears on its own.
Skimming the surface speeds it up. Then reconsider the product: a quat that foams
is also being consumed by your free chlorine, so you are paying twice.

<ProductBlock id="skimmer-sock" />

**If it keeps returning — raise calcium hardness.** This is the real fix for
persistent foam, and it is covered in the tip below. Target 200–300 ppm. Add
calcium chloride in small increments and retest between doses; you cannot take it
back out except by draining and diluting, and overshooting trades foam for
[scale and cloudy water](/problems/milky-white-pool-water).

<ProductBlock id="calcium-hardness-increaser" />

**If chlorine is falling too — treat it as algae.** Foam is the early symptom,
not the problem.

**If it pops — it was bubbles.** Go to the plumbing.

<UncommonTip>
  **Foam that keeps coming back is a calcium hardness reading you have not
  taken.**

  Low calcium hardness does not cause foam. What it does is let foam form easily
  and persist — typically at 150 ppm and below, with 200–300 ppm suppressing it
  and making what does form dissipate faster.

  That makes stubborn foam a free readout on the one water imbalance that hides
  in plain sight. Almost every other chemistry problem announces itself by
  changing how the water looks: cloudy, green, milky, stained. Low calcium
  hardness does the opposite — the water goes *sparkling clean*. Nothing about
  the pool's appearance warns you, while soft, aggressive water quietly etches
  plaster and works on metal fittings.

  So the owner skims the foam, or waits out the algaecide, and never tests the
  number the foam was pointing at. The foam was not the problem. It was the only
  symptom a symptomless problem produced.
</UncommonTip>

## When to stop and call someone

- **Bubbles from the returns that persist after the water level is correct.**
  That is a suction-side leak, and air in the pump is not something to live with.
- **Foam that survives oxidation, correct calcium hardness, and no algaecide.**
  Something is entering the water that should not be.
- **Foam alongside chlorine you cannot hold.** Solve the chlorine problem first;
  the foam is downstream of it.
- **Anyone about to drain over this.** Foam is not a drain-and-refill problem,
  and an empty shell carries real structural risk.

Telling you to buy a $12 calcium test instead of a bottle of foam remover costs
us the sale. That is the trade.

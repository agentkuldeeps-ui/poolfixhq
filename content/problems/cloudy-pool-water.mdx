Cloudy pool water is the symptom with the worst advice attached to it, because
three unrelated problems produce the same look and the internet treats them as
one. Shock it, the advice goes. Add clarifier. Run the filter longer.

One of those is right for your pool. The other two waste a weekend.

## What you're actually looking at

Start by finding out whether the water is holding particles or making them.

**Filtration failure** — the filter can't remove what's in there. The tell is
time: you've been running the pump and nothing has changed in 48 hours. Check
the pressure gauge first, because a filter that's blocked and a filter that's
bypassing look identical from the deck.

**Chemistry creating cloudiness** — the water itself is dropping solids out of
solution. This one gets missed constantly. The tell is speed and timing: it
appeared within hours of adding something, usually after a pH rise, and the
water often has a slightly milky or bluish cast rather than a dirty one.

**Particulate too fine to trap** — real debris, just smaller than your filter's
capture size. Pollen, fine silt after a storm, dead algae after a treatment. The
tell is that the water improves slowly and steadily rather than not at all, and
it looks worse under the pool light at night than it does at noon.

Two things get called cloudy that aren't. **New plaster dust** looks exactly
like a chemistry problem for the first two to three weeks after a replaster and
resolves on its own with brushing. And **dead algae** during a green-pool
treatment turns the water gray — that's a pool clearing, not a pool with a new
problem. If you arrived here from a green pool, read [green pool
water](/problems/green-pool-water) first.

## Test these numbers first

These don't diagnose cloudiness. They tell you which of the three fixes applies,
which is the fork worth getting right.

| Test | What you want | What it rules in or out |
|---|---|---|
| Free chlorine | ≥ 1 ppm, or ≥ 2 ppm with CYA present | Zero means you're heading for algae, not just cloudiness |
| pH | 7.0–7.8 | Above 7.8 with high calcium is the carbonate-clouding setup |
| Calcium hardness | Per your surface type | High calcium plus high pH is chemistry, not filtration |
| Total alkalinity | 80–120 ppm | Drives how fast pH moves on you |
| Filter pressure | Within 8–10 psi of clean | Above that, the filter is the problem |

CDC's home-pool guidance sets free chlorine at a minimum of 1 ppm, or at least
2 ppm where cyanuric acid is in use, with pH from 7.0 to 7.8. Those are the
*home* figures — the widely repeated 7.2–7.8 range is the public-facility
standard, and most sites quote it at homeowners without noticing.

<UncommonTip>
  **Water can go cloudy because it's making the cloud, not holding it.**

  When pH and calcium hardness climb far enough, calcium carbonate stops
  staying dissolved and precipitates as microscopic crystals throughout the
  water column. That's carbonate clouding, and it looks like ordinary cloudy
  water with one difference: it appeared fast, usually within hours of a pH
  rise, a fresh plaster cure, or a heater running hard.

  It matters because every standard fix makes it worse or does nothing.
  Filtering doesn't help — the crystals form faster than they're removed.
  Clarifier doesn't help — there's no debris to gather. Shocking is actively
  counterproductive, because a shock dose can push pH higher and precipitate
  more.

  The fix is chemistry: bring pH down and the crystals redissolve, often within
  a day. The diagnostic that costs nothing is timing. Filtration problems take
  days to appear. Carbonate clouding shows up between one test and the next.

  If your water is chalky white rather than merely hazy, that's this mechanism
  running hard, and it has its own article: [milky white pool
  water](/problems/milky-white-pool-water).
</UncommonTip>

## The fix, cheapest first

### 1. Clean the filter and run the pump continuously. Free.

Backwash a sand or DE filter, or pull and hose a cartridge. Then leave the pump
running 24 hours.

*Why this works:* cloudiness is a filtration race — particles enter, the filter
removes them, and the water is clear when removal wins. A filter within 8–10 psi
of its clean pressure has lost much of its flow, and part-time running means
most of the water never passes through it. This alone clears a good share of
cloudy pools and costs nothing.

### 2. Correct pH before anything else. Under $30.

If pH is above 7.8, bring it down and wait a day before judging the water.

*Why this works:* pH governs both whether chlorine works and whether calcium
stays dissolved. Correct it first and you may find the cloudiness resolves with
no further treatment — and if it doesn't, everything you try next works better
at the right pH.

<SafetyWarning>
  Never mix pool chemicals. Add each one to the water separately, never to
  another chemical and never into a bucket that held one. Muriatic acid and
  chlorine together produce chlorine gas. Add acid to water, never water to
  acid, pour slowly into a return flow with the pump running, and keep your
  face out of the vapor.
</SafetyWarning>

### 3. Clarifier, if the particles are simply too fine. Under $30.

*Why this works:* clarifier is a coagulant. It gathers particles too small for
your filter into clumps large enough to catch, so the filter starts winning a
race it was losing. It's the right tool for pollen, fine silt, and the tail end
of an algae treatment.

It is the wrong tool for a blocked filter and useless against carbonate
clouding. Buying it before you've diagnosed is how people end up with three
bottles in the shed and cloudy water.

<ProductBlock id="clarifier" />

### 4. Flocculant, only if the water is opaque. Under $150 including the water you'll lose.

*Why this works:* flocculant sinks everything to the floor in one pass instead
of gathering it for the filter. You then vacuum to waste — not through the
filter — which is what actually removes it.

Worth being blunt: this costs you a large volume of water, an hour of slow
manual vacuuming, and a refill that resets part of your chemistry. For a pool
you can still see the floor of, it's the wrong call. Reach for it when the water
is genuinely opaque.

<ProductBlock id="flocculant" />

### 5. Replace filter media, or call someone.

Sand that's years old, a cartridge that no longer comes clean, or DE grids with
tears will never clear a pool no matter what you pour in.

## How long it takes to clear

Assuming the pump runs continuously and the filter is actually working:

- **Slightly hazy** — 24 hours, mostly filtration.
- **Can't see the floor at the deep end** — 2 to 3 days.
- **Opaque** — a flocculant treatment and a vacuum-to-waste session, then a day.
- **Carbonate clouding** — often within a day of correcting pH, and no
  filtration time at all.

If nothing has changed after a week of continuous running, stop adding
chemicals. Something mechanical is wrong.

## What makes it come back

Short pump run times through summer is the most common cause, and it's the
cheapest to fix. Beyond that: a filter cleaned on a schedule rather than on
pressure, a pH that's allowed to drift up between tests, and a fill hose that
runs unattended and drops the whole pool's chemistry in one go.

If the water clouds *and* greens, the diagnosis changes — see [green pool
water](/problems/green-pool-water). If it clouds every time you shock, the
chemistry underneath is the issue, and [chlorine
basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) is the place to start.

## When to stop and call someone

- **Filter pressure won't rise at all**, or drops to zero at startup. Often a
  dead gauge, sometimes a bypassing filter — worth confirming before buying
  media.
- **Sand or DE returning to the pool.** That's a cracked lateral or standpipe
  inside the filter, and it needs the tank opened.
- **Water clears then clouds again within days**, repeatedly, with correct
  chemistry. Something is feeding it.
- **The pump won't hold prime**, so nothing circulates. Start at [pool pump not
  priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming).
- **Any electrical fault at the pad** — a breaker that trips, a warm or buzzing
  motor. Stop there.

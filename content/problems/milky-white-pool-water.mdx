Milky white pool water is the one clarity problem where the usual advice does
active harm. Every instinct says add chlorine, and chlorine does nothing here —
there is nothing alive in the water. What you are looking at is calcium that was
dissolved yesterday and is not dissolved today, crystallized into particles fine
enough to hang in the water column and scatter light.

That distinction sets up the thing most pages get wrong. Filtering the crystals
out and stopping them forming are two different jobs. Almost every article you
will find describes the first and stops. If the water is still oversaturated when
you finish, the pool goes white again on the next warm week.

## What you're actually looking at

Several different problems all get called cloudy, and they need opposite fixes.
Sort yours before you spend anything.

**Calcium precipitate** — uniform white or chalky, top to bottom, the same
density at the shallow end as the deep end. Surfaces feel gritty or rough under
your hand rather than slippery. This article.

**Dead algae after a shock treatment** — gray or blue-white rather than chalky,
and it follows a green phase. That is a pool clearing, not a pool with a new
problem. See [green pool water](/problems/green-pool-water).

**General haze from weak filtration or low chlorine** — hazy rather than white,
still blue, and it developed over days rather than appearing between two tests.
The triage for that is [cloudy pool water](/problems/cloudy-pool-water).

**Air in the returns** — the whiteness is bubbles. It is densest right at the
return jets, thins out with depth, and disappears when the pump stops. That is a
suction-side air leak, and it starts at [pool pump not
priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming).

### The free test: hold a glass up to the light

Fill a clear glass from the pool and hold it to a window. Then leave it on a
counter overnight.

Milk-white throughout, with nothing settling out by morning, means calcium. The
crystals are too fine and too close to the density of water to fall out in a
glass.

A layer of sediment on the bottom by morning, with clearer water above it, means
organic material or debris. That is a filtration problem and you are in the
wrong article.

It takes ten seconds and no equipment, and it answers the only question that
determines what you buy.

## Why calcium falls out of solution

Water holds a finite amount of dissolved calcium carbonate. Push it past
saturation and the excess stops being dissolved — it crystallizes out as
microscopic white particles suspended throughout the pool. The same process
happening on a surface instead of in open water is scale.

Four things push water over that line:

**pH rising.** The biggest lever and the fastest. Calcium carbonate solubility
drops sharply as pH climbs, and the relationship is not linear — the last few
tenths of a point above 8.0 cost you far more headroom than the tenths below 7.6.

**Calcium hardness rising.** Hard fill water, years of cal-hypo shock, fresh
plaster curing, or simple evaporation, which removes water and leaves every bit
of calcium behind.

**Total alkalinity rising.** More carbonate in the water means more available to
combine with calcium, and high alkalinity also drags pH upward on its own between
tests.

**Water temperature rising.** This is the one nobody tests.

### Temperature is an input, not a comfort reading

Calcium carbonate has inverse solubility. Almost everything else you dissolve in
water dissolves better as it warms. Calcium carbonate does the opposite — the
warmer the water, the less it will hold.

Temperature is one of the inputs to the saturation index the industry uses to
judge whether water is scale-forming or corrosive, and it carries real weight. A
pool that sat perfectly balanced in May can be scale-forming in mid-August with
zero chemical change. Same pH, same calcium hardness, same alkalinity, white
water.

This is the answer to "nothing changed and it went white," and it is the reason
the answer is so hard to find. Nothing did change. The ceiling came down.

Practical version: write the water temperature beside your test results. If you
are diagnosing a white pool and you have not looked at a thermometer, you are
missing an input that moved 15 degrees since your last clear reading.

### The localized spike — "it went white the second I added something"

There is a second, faster version of this that has nothing to do with your
overall numbers.

Some products are extremely alkaline in their own right, and where they enter the
water they create a pocket of pH far above whatever the pool as a whole reads.

**Soda ash** — the standard pH increaser — produces a pocket near pH 10 at the
point of addition. Calcium crashes out inside that pocket before it disperses.

**Cal-hypo shock** does something worse. It carries a pH around 11 to 12 straight
from the bag, and it is a calcium compound, so it raises pH and adds calcium to
the same pocket at the same time. Double hit. This is why a pool that was fine on
Friday goes white during Saturday's shock and the owner concludes the shock
"stirred something up."

Here is the useful part, which most pages leave out: **a small localized
precipitation normally redissolves within minutes** as the pocket blends into the
rest of the pool. That is the water absorbing a temporary spike and recovering,
which is what balanced water is supposed to do.

If it does not clear, the pool was already sitting near saturation and the dose
only tipped it. The haze is not the problem — it is the symptom of a pool with no
margin left.

## Test these numbers first

These do not tell you the water is white. They tell you which branch of the fix
you are on.

| Test | Target | What it means here |
|---|---|---|
| pH | 7.2–7.6 for this problem | The highest-leverage input. Above 7.8 is the most likely cause and the cheapest to correct |
| Total alkalinity | 80–120 ppm | Above 120, pH climbs back on its own — this is why the haze returns |
| Calcium hardness | 200–400 ppm | Above 400 you have little headroom; around 600 and up, pH alone may not save you |
| **Water temperature** | Record it, do not judge it | An input, not a comfort reading. A rise since your last clear test is a cause in its own right |
| Free chlorine | ≥ 1 ppm, or ≥ 2 ppm with CYA present | Rules out an organic cause hiding behind the haze |

CDC's home-pool guidance puts free chlorine at a minimum of 1 ppm, or at least
2 ppm where cyanuric acid is in use, with pH from 7.0 to 7.8. For a pool that has
gone white, aim at the low end of that pH range rather than the middle.

### The hardness number that sends people to the wrong fix

This article is exactly where this mistake gets made, so it is worth being
blunt about it.

If you are quoting a hardness figure from your municipal water report, that is
**total hardness**. Your pool test kit reads **calcium hardness**, and it will
come in around 70 to 80% of the total, because total hardness includes magnesium
and magnesium is not what is clouding your pool.

Get this wrong and you send yourself down the expensive branch. A water report
saying 550 ppm has people planning a partial drain when their actual calcium
hardness is around 400 and a bottle of acid was the whole answer. Confirm calcium
hardness with a drop test before you make any decision that costs water.

## The fix, cheapest first

### 1. Wait 24 hours, if it appeared right after you added something. Free.

Do not add anything else. Run the pump and leave it alone.

*Why this works:* cal-hypo haze and soda-ash haze are commonly a temporary pH
spike, and the crystals it produced are ordinary filterable particles. The spike
disperses on its own and the filter removes what is left, usually within 12 to
24 hours.

**This is the diagnostic that splits the whole article.** If the water has not
cleared after 24 to 48 hours of normal filtration, the pool is genuinely
oversaturated, and no amount of filtering will fix it — you are removing crystals
at the same rate the water is making new ones. Stop filtering and start on pH.

### 2. Lower pH. Under $30.

Dose muriatic acid to bring pH toward 7.2. Work out the dose from your actual
volume, not a guess — the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) takes a minute.

*Why this works:* pH has more leverage over calcium solubility than any other
input you can change today. Bringing it down raises the ceiling back above your
calcium level, which puts suspended crystals back into solution and stops new
ones forming. It also restores your chlorine's effectiveness, which high pH had
been quietly destroying — see [chlorine
basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics).

Add acid slowly into a return flow with the pump running. Never dump it in one
spot.

<SafetyWarning>
  Never mix pool chemicals. Add each one to the water separately, never to
  another chemical and never into a bucket that held one. Muriatic acid and
  chlorine together produce chlorine gas. Always add acid to water, never water
  to acid. Pour slowly into a return flow with the pump running and keep your
  face out of the vapor. Acid fumes corrode metal — do not store it beside the
  heater or the electrical panel.
</SafetyWarning>

### 3. Filter aggressively. Free to under $30.

Run the pump 24/7 and clean or backwash whenever pressure climbs 8 to 10 psi
above clean. Expect to do it more than once.

*Why this works:* chlorine is irrelevant here. The filter is the only thing
removing calcium particles from the water, and it only removes what passes
through it, so part-time running means most of the pool never gets treated.

Worth knowing which filter you have, because it changes your timeline. These
crystals are near the lower limit of what media can catch, and they slip through
sand more easily than through a cartridge or DE grid. **Sand-filter pools clear
slowest**, sometimes by days. That is normal, not a sign the fix is failing.

### 4. Flocculant, if the water is opaque. Under $30.

Only after pH is corrected. Flocculant sinks the precipitate to the floor so you
can vacuum it **to waste** — not through the filter.

*Why this works:* you are physically removing calcium from the pool instead of
asking a filter to catch a particle at the edge of its capability. Vacuuming
through the filter just puts most of it back.

Honest cost note: this costs you a meaningful volume of water, an hour of slow
manual vacuuming, and a refill that resets part of your chemistry. For a pool you
can still see the floor of, it is the wrong call.

<ProductBlock id="flocculant" />

### 5. Partial drain and refill. Escalate.

Only when calcium hardness is genuinely too high to correct with chemistry —
roughly 600 ppm and up, or when acid will not hold pH down.

*Why this works:* there is no chemical that destroys calcium. Removing water and
replacing it with softer water is the only way to lower the number. How much you
need depends entirely on your fill water's own hardness, so test that first — in
hard-water regions it can arrive high enough to make full correction impossible.

Never drain a pool completely. An empty shell can float out of the ground under
groundwater pressure, and that is a five-figure repair. Drain in stages, refill
as you go, and check local restrictions before you start.

### What does not work

**Shocking.** There is nothing organic to oxidize, and cal-hypo actively makes it
worse by adding calcium and pH together.

**Algaecide.** There is no algae. It is a bottle of nothing for this problem.

**Clarifier, dose after dose.** It clumps particles for the filter, which helps
only once the water has stopped making new ones. In an oversaturated pool you are
bailing with the tap still running.

**Scale inhibitor or sequestrant.** Read the label carefully, because most pages
blur this: a sequestrant **prevents** calcium coming out of solution. It does not
dissolve haze that already exists. It is the right product for the week after you
fix this, and completely useless during it.

## Why it comes back

Clearing the water does not change the saturation that produced it. If the pool
is still oversaturated when the water looks fine, it goes white again on the next
warm week or the next dose of soda ash. This is the section nobody writes, and it
is the one that saves you the second treatment.

**Hold pH at the low end through summer**, around 7.2 to 7.4, rather than the
middle of the range. Summer is when temperature is already eating your margin.

**Pre-dissolve cal-hypo in a bucket of pool water**, or switch to liquid
chlorine, which adds no calcium at all. On a pool that is already high in
calcium, that switch alone can be the whole solution.

**Never dump soda ash in one spot with the pump off.** Broadcast it slowly with
water moving, or skip it — most pools that need pH raised regularly have an
aeration problem, not a chemistry one.

**Watch calcium hardness creep.** Topping off concentrates it, because
evaporation takes water and leaves calcium behind. A pool on hard fill water
climbs every summer whether you add anything or not.

**Accept that temperature alone can tip a borderline pool.** If your numbers sit
at the edge in spring, they are over the edge in August. Correct them while the
water is cool.

<UncommonTip>
  **Calcium does not crash out evenly. It comes out of the hottest water
  first — and the hottest water in your system is inside the heater.**

  Because calcium carbonate is less soluble as temperature rises, the saturation
  ceiling is lowest wherever the water is warmest. Not the pool as an average:
  the specific hot spots. Inside a gas heater's heat exchanger, across the plates
  of a salt chlorine generator, at a sunlit spillway or along a tile line baking
  in afternoon sun. Those places cross the line before the pool does.

  Two consequences most owners never connect.

  **A diagnostic.** Milky water that appears right after the heater has been
  running, with nothing added, points at saturation rather than at anything you
  did. You have your cause without further testing, and step 2 is your fix.

  **A warning worth more than the rest of the article.** If the pool has gone
  milky, the heater and the salt cell have already been scaling internally, where
  you cannot see it — they crossed the line weeks ago. Scale inside a heat
  exchanger restricts flow and cooks the unit; scale on cell plates cuts chlorine
  output until a perfectly good cell reads as failed and gets replaced.

  So open the salt cell and look at the plates. White crust between them is a
  free early warning that your water is oversaturated, available weeks before the
  pool itself turns white. Almost nobody looks until output drops, and by then
  the damage is done and a $20 acid rinse would have prevented it.
</UncommonTip>

## When to stop and call someone

- **The water goes milky again within days of clearing, repeatedly, with pH in
  range.** That is a saturation problem that needs a proper index calculation
  against your actual temperature and hardness, and possibly partial drainage.
- **Calcium hardness above roughly 600 ppm.** Correcting that means removing
  water, and how much depends on your fill water's hardness. Getting the math
  wrong here is expensive in gallons.
- **Visible scale on tile, on the salt cell, or inside the heater.** Descaling a
  heat exchanger is a service job, and running a scaled heater damages it
  further every hour.
- **The pool will not hold pH down** no matter how much acid goes in. Something
  is driving it — usually alkalinity or aeration — and guessing at it gets
  costly.
- **Any consideration of a full drain.** Hydrostatic pop-out is real and it
  totals pools. Get someone who knows your water table involved before the water
  goes anywhere.

Most of this article talks you out of buying things — the acid that usually fixes
this costs less than the clarifier that will not, and we earn nothing on either.
That is the trade, and we would rather you kept the money.

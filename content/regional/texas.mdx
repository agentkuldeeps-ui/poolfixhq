Most state pool guides are the same article with a place name swapped in. Texas
pool care genuinely isn't, because the advice inverts inside the state line —
what's correct in Dallas is wrong in Lubbock, and both are Texas.

## The split that matters: your fill water

Texas water is hard almost everywhere. The state average runs around 11.9 grains
per gallon against a US average of 6.3, and every major city surveyed comes in
hard or very hard. But "hard" covers a range wide enough to reverse the advice.

| Metro | Fill water, total hardness | What that means for a plaster pool |
|---|---|---|
| Dallas | ~7.9 gpg (~135 ppm) | Arrives **below** target — hardness usually needs raising |
| Houston | ~8.0 gpg (~137 ppm) | Same. Adding calcium is normal here |
| Fort Worth | ~8.8 gpg (~150 ppm) | Below target, less so |
| Austin | ~10.5 gpg (~180 ppm) | Close to the bottom of range |
| El Paso | ~11.4 gpg (~195 ppm) | In range on arrival |
| San Antonio | ~17–21 gpg (~290–360 ppm) | At or above target. Sources disagree — test yours |
| Amarillo / Midland | ~18.5 gpg (~316 ppm) | Above target. Scale management |
| Odessa | ~19.0 gpg (~325 ppm) | Above target |
| Lubbock | ~22.2 gpg (~380 ppm) | Hardest in the state. Scale is the whole job |

**Read those as total hardness, not calcium hardness.** Municipal reports give
total; your test kit reads calcium, which is roughly 70–80% of it because the
rest is magnesium. So a Dallas figure of ~135 ppm total means the calcium number
you actually measure comes in lower still — further below the 200–400 ppm range
plaster pools generally want.

That's the split. A Dallas plaster pool owner is adding calcium. A Lubbock owner
adding calcium is making an existing problem worse.

San Antonio is the one to be careful with: the two sources we checked disagree
by roughly 4 grains, which is enough to change what you do. Test your own fill
water rather than trusting either figure.

<UncommonTip>
  **Evaporation concentrates hardness, so a West Texas pool gets harder all
  summer even if you do nothing wrong.**

  Water leaves as vapor. Minerals don't. Every gallon that evaporates leaves its
  calcium behind in the pool, and every gallon of top-up water you add brings
  more in with it. In a metro filling at 19–22 gpg, that's a pool concentrating
  hardness twice over through the hottest months.

  Which means a calcium reading that was fine in April can be scaling the tile
  line by August without a single mistake in between. The mechanism is
  arithmetic, not neglect — and it's why "my water was balanced" and "my heater
  is scaled" are both true statements about the same pool.

  Two practical consequences. Test calcium hardness on a schedule through
  summer, not just at opening, because it moves in one direction. And when
  hardness does climb past workable, understand that no chemical removes it —
  dilution with softer water is the only route, which is a different
  conversation in a drought year.
</UncommonTip>

## Season length: the honest answer

There isn't a single Texas figure, and any site handing you one hasn't checked.
The state spans a range of climates wide enough that a number covering El Paso
and Houston would mislead both.

The more useful answer is that **season length isn't really a calendar
question — it's a water temperature one.** Algae growth slows sharply as water
cools and is close to dormant at the low end. Heater decisions, cover decisions
and how hard you have to work to hold chlorine all key off water temperature,
not the month on the wall.

So the practical version for Texas: most of the state runs long enough that
"closing" in the northern sense is often optional, and a thermometer tells you
more than a date ever will. Watch the water temperature and let it set your
routine.

## Chlorine demand and the sun

Long, bright seasons mean chlorine is under constant UV attack, which is what
makes cyanuric acid such a live issue here. Stabilizer protects chlorine from
sunlight and simultaneously holds it back from working, and every stabilized
tablet adds more that only leaves with water.

Run tablets all season in a Texas pool and cyanuric acid creeps up quietly until
the pool stops holding chlorine at any sensible dose. See [free vs total vs
combined chlorine](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) for what the numbers mean, and
[cloudy pool water](/problems/cloudy-pool-water) if high calcium and high pH are
already showing up as haze.

<SafetyWarning>
  Never mix pool chemicals. Add each product to the water separately — never to
  another chemical and never into a bucket that held one. Chlorine and acid
  together produce chlorine gas. Add acid to water, never water to acid, and
  keep your face out of the vapor.
</SafetyWarning>

## Draining: municipal, not statewide

There is no single Texas rule, and this is the part most regional pages get
wrong by inventing one.

At state level, `25 Tex. Admin. Code § 265.188` requires backwash and drain
water to discharge through an air gap, the pipe opening at least two pipe
diameters above the receiving point. That chapter is written for public and
semi-public pools, and we can't confirm how far it reaches into residential
backyards — so treat it as context rather than as your rule.

**Your rule is your city's.** Three real examples, and they differ:

- **Universal City** — water must be dechlorinated and pH brought below 7.0
  before draining, and you're asked to call the city first.
- **Oak Point** — prohibits water above **0.1 ppm chlorine** entering the storm
  sewer.
- **Burleson** — no chlorinated water, saline water, or backwash into a street
  or gutter, because storm drains reach creeks and lakes untreated.

The common thread across all of them: **dechlorinate before you discharge, and
never into a storm drain.** 0.1 ppm is the recurring limit. Saltwater pools get
named separately in several ordinances, so don't assume salt water is treated as
harmless.

Call your own city before you drain. The five-minute phone call is cheaper than
the fine, and in a drought year some municipalities restrict draining entirely.

## When to stop and call someone

- **Scale you can feel on the tile line or inside the heater.** Removing it is
  acid work on equipment that's expensive to get wrong.
- **Hardness past workable with a drought restriction in force.** Dilution is
  the only fix and you may not be permitted to do it — that's a conversation
  with a local pro, not a product.
- **Slab or deck movement.** Texas clay soils move a great deal seasonally, and
  new cracks in a shell or a lifting coping stone are structural.
- **Anything gas or electrical at the pad.** Licensed work.

We'd earn more selling you a scale-removal product than telling you a Lubbock
pool may need dilution instead. The dilution is still the answer.

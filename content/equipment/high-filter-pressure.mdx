Nearly every page about this gives you a number to backwash at and stops. The
number is close to useless on its own, because filter pressure is high or low
only relative to what *your* filter reads when it's clean — and most owners have
never recorded that.

<SafetyWarning>
  Read this before you open anything. Hayward's filter manual states that pool
  and spa water circulation systems "operate under hazardous pressure during
  start up, normal operation, and after pump shut off," and that failure to
  follow the safety instructions "could result in violent separation of pump
  and/or filter components."

  Before servicing, all system and pump controls must be off **and** the filter's
  manual air relief valve must be open. Do not operate the system unless the
  filter access dome is fully engaged in the filter body.

  When restarting: open the air relief valve fully first, and don't close it
  "until a steady stream of water (not air or air and water) is discharged."

  Two more from the same manual: never operate or test the system above 50 psi,
  and **never purge the system with compressed air** — Hayward warns it "can
  cause components to explode, with risk of severe injury or death to anyone
  nearby." Use a low-pressure, high-volume blower if air purging is needed.

  A filter is a pressure vessel. High pressure is exactly when people decide to
  open one, which is exactly when this matters most.
</SafetyWarning>

## Your clean starting pressure is the whole measurement

Filter pressure has no universal normal. A healthy system might sit at 10 psi or
at 25 depending on plumbing, filter type, filter size and pump speed. What makes
a reading meaningful is the difference from your own clean baseline.

To get it: clean or backwash the filter properly, restart the pump, bleed the air
out until water runs steady from the relief valve, and read the gauge.

Then look at the gauge itself. **Most have two movable arrows on the bezel, and
almost nobody uses them.** Hayward's own start-up instruction is to "line up
green arrow on gauge to clean pressure" — and the red arrow marks the backwash
point. The manufacturer built a place to record your baseline directly onto the
instrument, because the reading is meaningless without it. Set the green arrow
now and the gauge answers the question by itself from then on.

Without it you're guessing, and this page can't help you much. With it, every
future reading has meaning.

## When to backwash — and an honest disagreement

Here the sources don't agree, and it's worth saying so rather than picking one.

| Source | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Hayward support site | Backwash when pressure rises **8-10 psi** over clean starting pressure |
| Hayward S200 sand filter manual | Backwash when the gauge is **6-8 psi** higher than the initial clean pressure |

Same manufacturer, two documents, two answers. We're not going to pretend one is
authoritative — the range across both is roughly 6 to 10 psi above baseline, and
where you land inside it isn't the important part.

What both agree on is the part everyone skips: **the threshold is measured from
your clean starting pressure.** Any advice that gives you an absolute psi number
without asking what your filter reads clean is telling you something it cannot
know.

On a sand filter, Hayward's procedure is: stop the pump, set the valve to
BACKWASH, start the pump and backwash "approximately 2 minutes or less depending
on dirt accumulation until water in sight glass is clear," then RINSE for about
half a minute to a minute before returning to FILTER.

One instruction worth obeying exactly: **always shut the pump off before moving
the filter control valve.** The manual says so twice, and changing valve
positions under pressure is how valve seats get destroyed.

## When high pool filter pressure isn't dirt, and cleaning doesn't help

This is the part missing from almost every other page, and it's where the real
diagnosis starts. If you've cleaned properly and the needle barely moved, stop
cleaning — you're treating the wrong thing. Hayward gives three other causes.

**Algae clogging the media.** Hayward is explicit that algae in the pool can clog
the filter and hold pressure high, and that the filter cannot eliminate algae by
itself. That's a chemistry problem wearing an equipment costume. The tell is
pressure that climbs back within days of every clean while the water still isn't
right — see [green pool water](/problems/green-pool-water) for the treatment, and
[cloudy pool water](/problems/cloudy-pool-water) if it's haze rather than color.

**A blocked or plugged return line.** Restriction downstream of the filter raises
pressure just as effectively as restriction inside it. Check return-side valve
positions first, because a diverter left half-turned after vacuuming is common
and free to fix. If the pump is also struggling to hold prime, that's a
suction-side problem instead — [pump not
priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming) covers it.

**The pump is too big for the filter.** Covered below — this is the one nobody
tells you.

**Media that needs replacing rather than rinsing.** Sand and cartridges don't
last forever. Backwashing removes debris; it doesn't restore media that has
degraded, channeled, or calcified. Replacement specifications are model-specific
— the S200, for example, takes 200 lbs of No. 22 silica sand at 0.45-0.55 mm with
the sand surface about six inches below the top of the tank — so work from your
own filter's manual rather than a general figure.

<UncommonTip>
  **A filter can read high pressure the day it's installed, with spotless media,
  if the pump is too big for it.**

  Hayward says it plainly on their own support site: if pressure keeps running
  high with reduced flow to the pool, "the pump may be moving more water than the
  filter is designed to handle," and you should check the flow ratings of both.

  The reason is that pressure across a filter is a function of *flow rate through
  the media*, not only of how loaded that media is. Push more gallons per minute
  through a given filter area and resistance rises whether the sand is filthy or
  brand new. So a correctly maintained filter on an oversized pump reads high
  permanently.

  Which puts the owner on a treadmill. Every page says high pressure means dirty,
  so they clean it. The needle barely moves. They clean it again, more often,
  eventually replacing media that was fine. Nothing works, because the problem
  was never dirt.

  There's a neat symmetry worth noticing here. The most common expensive mistake
  when returns are weak is buying a bigger pump — and a bigger pump is precisely
  what creates this. Two opposite symptoms, [weak
  flow](/equipment/low-pool-flow) and high pressure, and the same wrong purchase
  sits behind both.
</UncommonTip>

A dirty filter can also stop a heater lighting — the heater's flow switch is
downstream of everything on this page. [Heater will not
fire](/equipment/pool-heater-not-heating) covers that link.

## Why it comes back

**Because filters get dirty. That's the job.** A steady climb between cleans is
normal and is what the gauge is for. Only the rate is diagnostic.

**Fast returns to high pressure mean something is loading the filter.** Days
rather than weeks points at a bloom or at heavy debris, not at the filter.

**Media ages.** Sand rounds off and channels over years; cartridges lose pleat
integrity. Cleaning intervals shortening year on year with no other change is
the signal.

**Nothing changed except the pump.** New pump, new speed setting, or a
variable-speed unit run at a higher RPM will all raise pressure legitimately.
The [pool volume calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) helps you work out
the turnover you actually need before running faster than necessary.

On a salt pool, low flow also shows up as a No Flow error — [salt cell throwing
an error](/equipment/salt-cell-errors).

## When to stop and call someone

- **Pressure stays high after a proper clean, a media check and a valve check.**
  That's the sizing conversation, and it needs someone to compare the pump's flow
  rating against the filter's rather than sell you more chemicals.
- **Pressure that spikes suddenly rather than climbing.** A sudden jump is a
  blockage, not accumulation.
- **Any damage, cracking or distortion to the tank, clamp band or dome.** Stop
  using it. This is the component the "violent separation" warning is about.
- **Pressure won't come down and flow is dropping too** — start at [weak flow
  from the returns](/equipment/low-pool-flow), which sorts restriction by which
  side of the pump it's on. If it needs hands on it, [pool
  repair](/pool-repair).

The money-wasting move here is replacing media because the pressure is high.
Sand and cartridge replacement is one of the more expensive routine jobs on a
pool, and if the cause is sizing or a blocked return line, fresh media reads
just as high the day it goes in. Confirm the cause first. Saying so costs us the
media referral, and it's still the right order.

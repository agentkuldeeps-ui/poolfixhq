Almost everything written about air bubbles in the returns treats it as one
problem with one answer: you have a suction-side leak, here are eight places to
check. That's right about half the time, and it sends the other half of readers
crawling around dry unions for an afternoon.

The question that splits it takes ten seconds to answer.

## Start here: does the pump hold its prime?

Look at the pump lid while it runs.

**If the basket won't stay full, or bubbles stream steadily across the lid
window** — you have air being drawn in ahead of the impeller. That's a
suction-side leak, and finding it is a specific procedure we've already written
out step by step. Go to [pool pump not priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming),
work the checklist in order, and ignore the rest of this page. The lid o-ring is
the usual answer.

**If the basket sits full and clear, the pump runs quietly, and bubbles still
come out of the returns** — that's this page. The list is much shorter, and
nobody writes about it.

One more quick fork before the list: **if the returns are also weak**, the pump
is being starved rather than merely aerated, and the filter gauge will tell you
which side the restriction is on — [weak flow from the
returns](/equipment/low-pool-flow) covers that. If it's also loud, a starved
pump has a distinctive sound, covered in [why your pool pump is
loud](/equipment/pool-pump-loud-noise).

## Air bubbles in the returns when the pump primes fine

Work these in order. The first two are free.

### 1. Water level

If the water drops far enough that the skimmer gulps between waves, the pump
draws air in bursts and it exits at the returns. The tell is that it's
**intermittent** — it comes and goes with surface movement rather than running
steady.

Keep the level around the middle of the skimmer opening. This is the single most
common cause of bubbles on a pump that otherwise behaves, and it's free to fix.

### 2. Recent service or a recent refill

Air trapped in the plumbing during work usually clears within the first several
minutes of running, longer on a system with a lot of pipe. If it hasn't gone
after a full cycle or two, something was left unsealed rather than left full —
and you're back to the leak procedure.

### 3. A return fitting that's aerating on purpose

Point a return eyeball up toward the surface and it drags air down with the jet.
That's agitation at the surface, not gas in your plumbing, and the giveaway is
that it stops when you aim the eyeball down. Worth thirty seconds before
assuming a fault.

### 4. A salt cell

This one deserves its own section, because the standard advice about it is
unsupported.

## The salt cell question

Search this and you'll be told, confidently and everywhere, that a salt chlorine
generator produces small bubbles and they're perfectly normal.

We went looking for a manufacturer saying that. We didn't find one.

The Hayward AquaRite manual doesn't mention gas bubbles at all. The Pentair
IntelliChlor guide doesn't describe visible bubbling as a normal operating
condition either. What it does contain is a warning, and it points the other
way — that the generator must never be run without proper flow or water
circulation, because a build-up of flammable gases will result in hazardous
conditions.

There's a second warning worth knowing, because it's rarely repeated anywhere:
the power center has to be wired to the load side of the timer or relay, so the
cell can only receive power when the pump is running. Wired wrong, a cell can
generate with no water moving at all.

Hayward's manual describes the flow switch as the interlock that's supposed to
prevent exactly this — when it detects no flow, the unit stops generating. It
also specifies at least a 12-inch straight pipe run upstream of the switch,
which matters because a switch installed too close to a fitting can read flow
inaccurately.

So here's the honest position. **We can't tell you the bubbles are harmless,
because no manufacturer document we found says that.** What we can tell you is
that gas at the cell is real enough that manufacturers write warnings about it,
and that the dangerous version of it happens when the cell runs without adequate
flow. Bubbles and poor flow travel together often enough that "it's just the
salt cell" is the one conclusion that stops you checking the thing worth
checking.

If you have a salt system and bubbles: confirm flow is genuinely good, confirm
the flow switch is working, and confirm the cell is wired so it can't run
without the pump.

## What the basket and the gauge add

Two readings worth taking before you conclude anything.

**The pump basket, while running.** Full and clear means the suction side is
sealed and you're in this article. Bubbles crossing the lid window means you're
in the other one. This is the single most informative thing you can look at, and
it costs nothing.

**The filter pressure gauge.** Bubbles with normal pressure and normal flow is a
different situation from bubbles with pressure well below your usual baseline —
the second says the pump isn't getting enough water, which is a restriction or a
leak rather than a stray pocket of air.

## Why it comes back

**Water level drifts.** Evaporation through a hot week is enough to take a pool
from fine to gulping, so a bubble problem that reappears every summer and
disappears after rain is a level problem, not an equipment one.

**O-rings dry out.** The pump lid o-ring needs to stay lubricated and it
perishes with age and sun. It's the most common single entry point for air, and
it's a few dollars.

**Run time and turnover.** If you shortened the pump schedule to save power and
the returns started behaving differently, that's related — work out the turnover
you actually need with the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) before trimming further. On a salt
pool, shorter run time also means less generating time, so the chlorine side
suffers too; [chlorine basics](/chemistry/chlorine-basics) covers what that
costs you.

## When to stop and call someone

- **The pump won't hold prime and you've worked the whole checklist.** An
  invisible suction leak is often underground, and locating it is a pressure-test
  job with equipment most owners don't have. Start at [pool
  repair](/pool-repair).
- **A salt cell that can energize while the pump is off**, or a flow switch you
  suspect isn't cutting the cell. That's an electrical and safety issue, not a
  maintenance one, and the manufacturer warnings above are the reason.
- **Bubbles plus a pressure reading far below your baseline plus noise.** The
  pump is being starved, and running it that way damages seals.
- **Bubbles that survive a correct water level, a new lid o-ring, and a full
  run cycle after service.**

The money-wasting move here is replacing a salt cell because the water looks
fizzy. Cells are among the more expensive parts on the pad, they fail in ways
that show up as low chlorine output rather than bubbles, and swapping one to
chase this symptom usually changes nothing. Check the level and the lid first —
between them they're a few dollars and they explain most of these. Saying so
costs us the referral on the expensive part.

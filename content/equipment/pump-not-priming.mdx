A pool pump not priming is an air problem, not a power problem. The motor's
spinning, the basket won't fill, and the temptation is to assume something
expensive has died. It usually hasn't.

Everything between the skimmer and the impeller is under vacuum when the pump
runs. Any gap on that stretch lets air in instead of water out, and a pump that's
pulling air can't build the column of water it needs to catch.

## What the symptom is reporting

Priming means filling the pump housing with water so the impeller has something
to grip. An impeller spinning in air moves nothing — it needs water to create
the pressure differential that pulls more water behind it.

So a pump that won't prime is telling you one of two things. Either **air is
getting in** on the suction side and breaking the vacuum as fast as it forms, or
**water can't get to it** because something is blocking the path or a valve is
shut.

That's the whole diagnosis. Everything below is working out which, in the order
that costs least.

## The sequence the pump runs

Worth understanding before you start pulling things apart, because it tells you
where to look:

1. Water sits in the skimmer and main drain lines.
2. The impeller spins and drops pressure inside the housing.
3. Atmospheric pressure pushes water up the suction line into that low-pressure
   space.
4. The housing fills, the basket floods, and the pump catches.
5. Water leaves under pressure toward the filter.

Step 3 is the fragile one. It relies on the suction side being sealed. A leak
anywhere on it means the pump pulls the easy thing — air — instead of the hard
thing, which is a column of water lifted against gravity.

### Priming it properly

Most failed priming attempts are the procedure, not a fault. Worth doing this
correctly once before you start diagnosing:

Cut power at the breaker. Open the pump lid and fill the pot to the top with a
hose — not halfway, to the rim, until it spills. That water is what the impeller
grips on startup; a half-filled pot gives it nothing to work with.

Check the o-ring seat while the lid is off. The groove should be clean and free
of grit, and the o-ring should sit in it without being pinched or standing proud
at any point. A single grain of sand on that face is a leak path. Wipe both
surfaces, run a thin film of silicone lubricant around the o-ring, and seat it.

Tighten the lid hand-tight only. Wrenching it down distorts the o-ring and makes
the seal worse, not better — this is the most common way people create the
problem they're trying to fix.

Open every suction valve and at least one return. A pump trying to push into a
closed system won't prime even with perfect suction.

Then power up and watch.

### What a real prime looks like, and what a false one looks like

These are easy to confuse, and telling them apart saves you diagnosing a fault
that isn't there.

**A real prime:** the basket fills within thirty seconds to two minutes and the
water in it goes *still* — clear, with the level steady above the basket. The
motor note drops and deepens as it takes on load. Filter pressure climbs and
settles at a number. Water leaves the returns in a steady push.

**A false prime:** the pot has water in it, so it looks caught, but the water is
churning and full of bubbles and never clears. The motor stays high and thin,
because it isn't loaded — it's moving air and water together. Filter pressure
sits low or doesn't move. The returns pulse rather than push.

The motor note is the tell most people miss. A loaded pump sounds heavier than
an unloaded one, and once you've heard the difference you can diagnose from the
back door. If the pot has water but the pitch never drops, you have air coming
in and the pump has not primed — keep working the list below rather than
assuming it's fine.

If it hasn't caught within five minutes, shut it off. Running dry destroys the
mechanical seal in minutes, and that turns an eleven-dollar o-ring job into a
stripped-down pump.

## Check in this order

### 1. Water level and valves. Free, thirty seconds.

**What to check:** water at least halfway up the skimmer opening, and every
suction valve open.

**Good:** skimmer throat submerged, valves aligned to skimmer and main drain.

**Bad:** water below the skimmer mouth means the pump is drawing air directly —
this is the single most common cause and it costs nothing to fix. A valve closed
after a filter clean is the second most common.

### 2. The pump lid, its o-ring, and the basket. Under $30.

**What to check:** lid o-ring seated, clean and supple. Lid tightened hand-tight
with the sealing face clean. Basket not cracked.

**Good:** the o-ring is round in cross-section, flexible, with no flat spots, and
it stays in its groove when you handle the lid.

**Bad:** flattened, hardened, cracked, or squared-off in cross-section. Roll it
between your fingers — a good one springs back, a spent one stays deformed. So is
any o-ring you've been greasing with petroleum jelly; petroleum swells and
softens the rubber over time until it no longer holds its shape in the groove.
Use silicone lubricant.

Also check the lid itself. A hairline crack in clear polycarbonate is nearly
invisible dry and shows as a dark line when the pot is full. And check that the
o-ring hasn't been stretched — one that's been rolled off and on repeatedly grows
slightly and then won't sit flat.

*Why this works:* the lid is the largest opening on the suction side and the only
one you open regularly. It's where the seal gets disturbed, dried out and
pinched. This is the answer more often than everything below it combined.

### 3. The suction lines, unions and diverter valve.

**What to check:** every joint between the skimmer and the pump — union nuts,
threaded fittings, the diverter valve body and its stem seal.

**Good:** a full basket that stays full, with no bubbles crossing the lid.

**Bad:** a steady stream of small bubbles through the lid window while the pump
runs. Distinguish this from the burst of air you get in the first thirty seconds
of any startup, which is normal and clears. Air that keeps arriving at the same
rate after two minutes is a leak.

The diverter valve is a frequent culprit because its stem seal is a wear part
nobody inspects. Threaded fittings are the next most likely, particularly any
that were sealed with tape rather than thread compound, and any joint that has
been apart for a repair. Union nuts loosen with thermal cycling — snug them by
hand and see whether the bubbles change.

### 4. Impeller and skimmer blockage.

**What to check:** with the breaker off, reach in and feel for debris in the
impeller vanes. Check the skimmer basket and the line beneath it.

**Good:** vanes clear, impeller turns freely by hand.

**Bad:** packed leaves, a hair tie, a bottle cap. A partially blocked impeller
often still primes but moves little water, which is a different symptom people
mistake for the same one — the pot fills and clears normally, but filter pressure
comes up low and the returns are weak.

The skimmer line matters as much as the impeller. A basket packed solid with
leaves starves the pump the same way a blocked impeller does, and it's the one
place to check first because it costs nothing and takes ten seconds.

### 5. Check valve, if the pump sits above water level.

**What to check:** the flapper holding water in the suction line when the pump
stops.

**Bad:** a failed check valve drains the line back to the pool every time the
pump shuts off, so it has to lift a full column of water on every start. That
presents as "primes eventually, and struggles."

<UncommonTip>
  **A suction-side leak never leaves a puddle. That's why people can't find it.**

  On the pressure side — pump to filter to returns — a leak pushes water out,
  and you get a wet spot to follow. On the suction side, everything is under
  vacuum, so a leak pulls air *in*. Nothing comes out. There's nothing to see,
  nothing to feel, and no drip to trace.

  So the instinct to hunt for water is not just unhelpful, it's actively
  misleading: any water you do find is on the pressure side, which is not what's
  stopping your pump from priming. People spend a weekend chasing a damp union
  that was never the problem.

  Invert the test. Instead of looking for water escaping, look for air entering
  — bubbles in the pump lid window while it runs, and the point where they start.
  And if you find an actual wet spot, note it for later and go back to the
  suction side, because you've just ruled that leak out.
</UncommonTip>

## Parts you may need

Priming failures live at the cheap end of the parts catalog, which is the whole
reason to work in this order.

- **Pump lid o-ring** — the most likely single part.
- **Silicone lubricant** — never petroleum jelly.
- **Diverter valve stem seal or o-ring kit** — if bubbles trace to the valve.
- **Filter pressure gauge** — not a priming part, but a dead gauge makes every
  step after this harder to read.
- **Mechanical seal kit** — only if the pump has already run dry and started
  leaking from between the motor and the wet end.

## What it costs to have done

So the DIY-versus-pro call is an informed one rather than a guess:

- **Service call to diagnose and reseal** — typically a single labor hour plus
  parts, and the tech will usually find it faster than you will because they've
  seen it a hundred times.
- **Suction-side leak detection** — pressure-testing equipment, priced as a
  specialist job, and worth it once you've exhausted the visible joints.
- **Pump replacement** — the outcome you're trying to avoid, and the one a lot
  of owners are sold before anyone checked an eleven-dollar seal.

The honest version: if the lid o-ring, water level and valves all check out and
you can't find bubbles, the next step is pressure testing, and that's a tool
problem rather than a skill problem. Paying someone for an hour beats buying a
pump you didn't need.

## When to stop and call someone

- **The pump has run dry** and is now leaking between the motor and the wet end.
  That's the mechanical seal, and continuing to run it takes out the bearings.
- **You've checked every accessible joint** and still have bubbles. The leak is
  underground or under the deck, and finding it needs pressure testing.
- **The motor hums but doesn't spin.** Different fault — usually a start
  capacitor, which holds a charge after the power is off and can injure you.
- **The breaker trips** when the pump starts. Stop. That's licensed electrical
  work.
- **Water level drops on its own** between top-ups. You may have a line leak
  rather than a seal problem, and that's a service call either way.

Pool pump loud noise is one of the few faults you can diagnose from thirty feet
away, because the three common causes sound nothing alike. The trouble is that
most advice treats "noisy pump" as one problem and sends everyone to the same
answer, which is usually "replace the motor."

Two of these three will destroy the pump if you keep running it. One is
harmless. Learning the difference takes about a minute.

## Identify the sound first

| What you hear | What it is | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Screech, squeal, or metallic grinding | Motor bearings failing | Stop running it |
| Gravel, marbles, or rocks tumbling | Cavitation — the pump is starved | Stop running it |
| Rhythmic slap, tick, or knock | Debris in the impeller | Fix at your convenience |
| Deep hum with no water movement | Not a noise fault — the motor isn't starting | Different article |
| Low drone that's always been there | Normal motor and plumbing vibration | Nothing wrong |

Two of those are commonly confused, and it's worth being precise: **bearings
sound like metal on metal. Cavitation sounds like the pump is full of stones.**
Bearing noise rises and falls with motor speed. Cavitation changes when you
change the water path — close a valve halfway, and cavitation shifts noticeably
while bearing noise doesn't care.

## How to listen properly

Diagnosing by ear works, but only if you isolate the two halves of the machine.
A pool pump is a motor bolted to a wet end, and each one makes its own noises.

Stand at the pad with the pump running and put your hand flat on the **motor
housing** — the finned barrel at the back. Then on the **wet end** — the plastic
volute at the front where the water is. Vibration that's strongest at the motor
is bearings. Vibration strongest at the wet end is water, which means cavitation
or debris.

For a clearer read, use a long screwdriver as a stethoscope: handle against your
ear, tip against the motor housing near the shaft end. Bearing noise comes
through as a distinct rasp. It's a genuinely old trick and it works better than
it has any right to.

Then run the isolation test. **Shut the pump off and listen to it spin down.**

- Noise that continues as the motor coasts, and fades with the shaft slowing, is
  **mechanical** — bearings.
- Noise that stops the instant the impeller stops moving water, before the motor
  has finished spinning, is **hydraulic** — cavitation or debris.

That single test separates the expensive fault from the cheap ones, and it costs
nothing.

<UncommonTip>
  **Failed bearings are usually a symptom, not the disease. The mechanical seal
  went first.**

  The mechanical seal sits on the pump shaft between the wet end and the motor,
  and its whole job is keeping pool water out of the motor. When it wears out it
  doesn't fail dramatically — it weeps. Water tracks along the shaft, past the
  slinger, and into the motor's front bearing, where it washes out the grease and
  starts corroding the race.

  Then, months later, the bearing starts screeching and everyone calls it a
  motor problem.

  This is why replacing the motor alone is often buying the same fault twice.
  The new motor goes onto the same shaft, behind the same worn seal, and the
  same water gets into the same place. **Replace the mechanical seal whenever
  you replace a motor for bearing noise**, and understand that if you don't know
  why the bearings died, the seal is the default answer.

  The early tell is a drip. A small puddle under the pump between the wet end
  and the motor, especially one that appears while running and stops when the
  pump stops, is a seal beginning to go. That is the cheap moment — a seal kit
  and an afternoon. Ignored, it becomes a motor and a seal kit, and the noise is
  how you find out you missed it.
</UncommonTip>

## Cause 1: cavitation — the pump is starved

Cavitation is a water supply problem that manifests as noise. The pump is trying
to move more water than the suction side can deliver, so pressure at the eye of
the impeller drops far enough that water flashes to vapor. Those vapor bubbles
travel into the higher-pressure part of the impeller and **implode**.

That implosion is what you're hearing. Each collapse is a microscopic shockwave,
and collectively they pit the impeller face and hammer the seal. Industrial pump
engineers treat cavitation as a destruction mechanism rather than a nuisance for
exactly this reason — sustained cavitation removes metal.

### What causes it

Every cause is a restriction on the suction side, or a pump asking for more than
the plumbing can give:

- **Blocked skimmer or pump basket.** The most common by a wide margin, and free
  to fix.
- **Water level too low**, so the skimmer is drawing part air.
- **Partially closed suction valve** — often after someone worked on the filter.
- **Clogged impeller**, restricting flow at the eye itself.
- **Undersized or too-long suction plumbing.** A pump moved or upsized without
  the pipe being upsized is a permanent cavitation source, not an intermittent
  one.
- **Pump too big for the system.** A 2 HP pump on 1.5-inch suction pipe will
  cavitate on a clean day.

### Fixing it, cheapest first

1. **Empty both baskets and check the water level.** Free, and it resolves most
   cases.
2. **Open every suction valve fully** and confirm nothing was left throttled.
3. **Clear the impeller** with the breaker off.
4. **Slow the pump down**, if you have a variable speed unit. Lower speed means
   lower suction velocity, which is the direct cure — this is the honest reason
   variable speed pumps solve noise complaints that new single-speed pumps don't.
5. **Look at the plumbing** if it cavitates at every speed with everything clean.
   That's a design problem, and no part replacement fixes it.

If a pump has been cavitating for a season, inspect the impeller when you get to
it. Pitting on the vane faces confirms the diagnosis and tells you the damage is
already done.

## Cause 2: bearings

Bearings are the fault everyone assumes and the one that's genuinely expensive.

**What it sounds like:** a rising screech as the motor comes up to speed, a
continuous metallic grind, or a rumble that gets worse over weeks. It tracks
motor speed, and it continues during spin-down.

**What to check:** with the breaker off, remove the pump lid and turn the motor
shaft by hand. A healthy shaft turns smoothly with no grit. A failing one feels
rough, catchy, or has play if you push it side to side.

**What to do:** bearings can be replaced, but on most residential pool motors the
labor makes a replacement motor the sensible choice. Before you order one, work
out **why** they failed — the tip above is the answer more often than not — and
replace the mechanical seal at the same time.

Do not keep running it. A seizing bearing takes the shaft with it, and a locked
rotor on a running motor is a fire risk rather than an inconvenience.

## Cause 3: debris in the impeller

The harmless one, and the one to rule out first because it's free.

**What it sounds like:** rhythmic. A tick, slap, or knock that repeats at
impeller speed rather than a continuous noise. It stops the moment water stops
moving.

**What to check:** breaker off. Remove the lid, remove the basket, and reach into
the impeller eye. Pebbles, acorns, hair ties and bottle caps all fit and all
rattle.

**Why it happens twice:** a cracked pump basket lets debris straight through to
the impeller. If you're pulling debris out repeatedly, the basket is the actual
fault.

## Cause 4: it's not the pump, it's the mounting

Worth checking before you buy anything, because it costs nothing and it's the
one nobody looks at.

A pump bolted directly to a hollow deck, a thin equipment pad, or a wooden
platform turns that surface into a soundboard. Rigid PVC run into the house wall
carries vibration structurally. In both cases the pump is fine and the noise is
transmission.

Tests: put your hand on the pad while it runs — if the slab is buzzing, that's
your amplifier. Slip something dense and compliant under the pump feet
temporarily and see whether the noise drops. If it does, proper vibration pads
and a flexible section in the plumbing solve it permanently for very little
money.

<SafetyWarning>
  Cut power at the breaker before opening a pump, and confirm it's off rather
  than assuming. A pump on a timer or automation can start on its own with no
  warning while your hand is in the impeller. If the motor has been running,
  the housing will be hot enough to burn.
</SafetyWarning>

## What it costs to have done

So the DIY-versus-pro call is informed:

- **Debris in the impeller** — free, ten minutes, no tools beyond a screwdriver.
- **Basket or lid o-ring** — parts-only, and the cheapest tier on the pad.
- **Mechanical seal kit** — an afternoon and a modest parts cost if you're
  comfortable splitting the pump. Requires pulling the wet end from the motor.
- **Motor replacement** — the expensive outcome, and the one worth confirming
  before you commit. Always with a new seal.
- **Re-plumbing an undersized suction line** — a real job, and the only fix for
  design cavitation. Get a quote before assuming a new pump helps, because a new
  pump on the same pipe cavitates the same way.

## When to stop and call someone

- **Grinding that's getting worse week over week.** A seizing bearing is a fire
  risk, not just a repair.
- **The motor is hot enough that you can't hold your hand on it.** Overheating
  alongside noise means bearings or windings.
- **Any burning smell**, or a breaker that trips when it runs. Stop. Electrical.
- **Cavitation at every speed with everything clean.** That's a plumbing design
  problem and it needs someone who can size the suction side properly.
- **A pump that has run dry.** Assume seal damage even if it sounds fine now.

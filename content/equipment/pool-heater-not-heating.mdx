A pool heater is the last thing in the chain and the first thing to get blamed.
It sits downstream of the pump and the filter, it won't light unless several
conditions are all true at once, and when it refuses it usually isn't reporting
its own failure — it's reporting one of theirs.

So work the prerequisites before you touch the heater.

<SafetyWarning>
  **If you smell gas: don't operate any switch, don't restart anything, leave the
  area, and call your gas utility from somewhere else.**

  This page covers what an owner can safely check — water flow, filter pressure,
  valve positions, whether the gas shutoff is open, whether the thermostat is
  calling for heat. It stops there deliberately.

  Do not disassemble the gas train, adjust gas pressure, or clean burners or gas
  jets on a live appliance. Combustion work is licensed work in most
  jurisdictions, and getting it wrong is dangerous rather than merely
  ineffective.

  **Never bypass a pressure switch or a limit switch to make a heater run.**
  Those switches exist to stop it firing under conditions where firing is unsafe.
  A heater that won't light is doing its job.
</SafetyWarning>

## What has to be true before it lights

Every gas heater runs through the same logic, whatever the badge says. It
confirms there's a call for heat, confirms water is moving through it, confirms
combustion conditions are safe, then ignites. Any link failing stops the
sequence, and where it stops is the diagnosis.

| Prerequisite | Proven by | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Call for heat | Thermostat setpoint above water temperature | Setpoint too low, or mode is off |
| Power | Electrical supply and connections | Breaker, or a loose connection |
| **Water flow** | **Water pressure switch** | **Pump off, low flow, dirty filter, valves** |
| Gas supply | Gas valve and supply pressure | Shutoff closed, supply issue |
| Safe combustion | Vent pressure and limit switches | Obstruction, venting, overheat |

Flow is the one that fails most, and it's the one an owner can actually
investigate.

## Heater will not fire: check these first, in order

**1. Is the pump running and primed?** No flow, no fire, by design. If the pump
isn't holding prime, that's a separate problem — [pump not
priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming) has it.

**2. What does the filter gauge say against its clean baseline?** This is the
highest-value check on the page and almost nobody makes it. See below for why.
[High filter pressure](/equipment/high-filter-pressure) covers reading it
properly.

**3. Valve positions.** A diverter left part-turned after vacuuming, or a heater
bypass valve closed, starves the heater specifically while the rest of the system
looks normal.

**4. Thermostat setpoint and mode.** Obvious, free, and genuinely the answer often
enough to check before anything else.

**5. Is the gas shutoff open?** Raypak's start-up list is explicit about
inspecting all valves that allow gas flow and confirming they're open, including
the gas line shutoffs and the heater's own gas valve switch. Checking a valve
position is fine. Going further into the gas train is not.

**6. Obvious obstruction around the unit.** Raypak notes that leaves and organic
debris accumulating inside a heater can restrict airflow or inhibit burner
combustion — and, more memorably, that spider webs around the heater may indicate
nests in critical areas like gas jets or burners, and that rodent nests are
highly flammable and must be removed before attempting ignition. If you find any
of that, it's a technician's job to clear it, not yours.

<UncommonTip>
  **Your heater may be reporting a filter problem.**

  A heater cannot light until its water pressure switch proves enough water is
  moving through it. That switch protects the heat exchanger — the expensive part
  — from taking heat that nothing is carrying away.

  Now the part that isn't obvious. Hayward's commissioning instruction for the
  Universal H-Series is to set flow rates above the minimum **"accounting for a
  dirty filter."** Read that again: the design assumption is that flow *falls* as
  the filter loads, and the installer is supposed to leave headroom for it.

  When that headroom is thin — a marginal pump, long plumbing, an undersized
  filter — the system passes on a clean filter and drops below the heater's
  minimum as the filter fills.

  That produces a pattern owners describe constantly and read exactly backwards:
  the heater is reliable right after a backwash, gets flaky over the following
  weeks, then works perfectly again the day after the next clean. It gets called
  an intermittent heater fault. It's a flow margin problem, and the heater has
  been telling the truth the whole time.

  The test costs nothing: note your filter pressure when the heater misbehaves,
  and note it again right after a clean. If the failures track the filter, stop
  looking at the heater.

</UncommonTip>

## Fault codes, and why they're their own articles

Modern heaters display a code rather than simply refusing. Hayward's Universal
H-Series — the H150FD, H200FD, H250FD, H300FD, H350FD, H400FD and H500FD —
shows a set that includes conditions tied to the water pressure switch, the vent
pressure switch and the temperature limit switch, among others.

Those codes are specific enough that decoding them properly means naming exact
model families and what each unit is sensing when it trips, which is more than
this page should carry. We're writing them up individually.

What matters here: **a code is the heater telling you where in its sequence it
stopped.** Note it before you power-cycle anything, because clearing the display
without recording the code throws away the most useful piece of information you
had.

## Why it comes back

**Flow margin doesn't improve on its own.** If the heater is dropping out as the
filter loads, it will keep doing that on the same cycle until the underlying
margin changes — a cleaner filter schedule, or a look at whether the pump and
filter are matched. [Weak flow from the returns](/equipment/low-pool-flow)
covers finding the restriction, and the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) is where to start if you're working
out whether your turnover and run time leave the heater any headroom at all.

**Seasonal first starts fail for seasonal reasons.** Nests, debris and drain
openings left open from winterizing are all on Raypak's list, and none of them
are present in July.

**Chemistry quietly eats the heat exchanger.** Water held at low pH strips copper
out of a heater's internals, and the first visible sign is often a teal stain in
the pool rather than anything at the equipment pad. If you've had that, the
heater has been corroding — [pool stain
identification](/problems/pool-stains-identification) explains the connection.

## When to stop and call someone

- **Any smell of gas, at any point.** Leave, then call the utility. Nothing else
  on this list matters.
- **The heater has cleared its flow prerequisite and still won't light.** That's
  inside the ignition and gas system, and it's licensed work.
- **A fault code that persists after flow and filter are confirmed good.** Record
  the code and the model number before calling — it shortens the visit.
- **Any nest, heavy debris or corrosion visible inside the cabinet.** Raypak
  flags rodent nests as highly flammable. Don't ignite it to find out.
- **Soot, scorching, or a yellow rather than blue flame.** Combustion problem,
  stop using it, [book someone](/pool-repair).

Worth saying plainly: the expensive mistake here is replacing a heater that was
never broken. Heaters are among the largest single costs on a pool, and a unit
that refuses to fire because flow is short will refuse identically when it's new.
Confirm the prerequisites first — filter, flow, valves — and if they're the
cause, you've saved thousands and a technician's visit. That advice costs us the
biggest referral on the site.

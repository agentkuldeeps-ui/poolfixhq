A pool pump not turning on feels terminal. Nothing happens, the water sits
still, and the obvious conclusion is that the motor has died. Most of the time
it hasn't — the electricity simply isn't arriving, or the part that gives the
motor its initial shove has quit.

The cheapest possible answer and the most expensive one produce the same silence
at the pad. So work the path in order rather than jumping to the end of it.

## Which silence are you dealing with

Before touching anything, stand at the pad and note exactly what the pump does
when it should be running. There are three distinct symptoms and they lead in
different directions:

| What it does | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Completely silent, no vibration | Power isn't reaching the motor. Work the path below |
| Hums but doesn't spin | Power is arriving. Suspect the capacitor, or a seized shaft |
| Starts, runs, then dies after a while | Thermal overload. See the tip below — this is its own diagnosis |
| Trips the breaker the instant it starts | Stop. This is a fault, not a puzzle |

That last one deserves emphasis: a breaker tripping on start is the electrical
system telling you something is wrong badly enough that it disconnected itself.
Don't reset it repeatedly to see if it holds.

## What has to happen for the motor to start

Understanding the chain makes the order below obvious rather than arbitrary.

Power leaves the breaker, passes through whatever controls the schedule — a
mechanical timer, a relay, or an automation panel — and reaches the motor's
terminal board. Inside the motor, a single-phase induction winding can't start
turning on its own. It needs a second winding, energized out of phase, to give
the rotor a directional push. **That phase shift is what the start capacitor
provides.**

Once the rotor is spinning, a centrifugal switch drops the start winding out and
the motor runs on its main winding alone. Which is why a motor with a dead
capacitor will often hum contentedly and never turn: everything is energized,
nothing is nudging it in a direction.

Break the chain anywhere and you get silence. The capacitor is the link that
fails most, and it costs a small fraction of what a motor does.

## Check in this order

### 1. The breaker, properly. Free.

**What to check:** the breaker at the sub-panel, and any GFCI in the circuit.

**Good:** the handle sits firmly in ON.

**Bad:** a tripped breaker often doesn't look tripped — the handle can sit in a
middle position that reads as ON at a glance. Push it fully to OFF, then back to
ON. That's the only way to reset it, and a surprising number of "dead pumps" end
here.

If it trips again immediately, stop and read the last section.

### 2. The timer or automation. Free.

**What to check:** whether the controller is actually calling for the pump.

**Good:** the timer's ON dog is engaged, or the automation shows the pump on.

**Bad:** mechanical timers drift and the dogs work loose, so the schedule quietly
stops matching what you think it is. Push the mechanism into an ON position by
hand. If the pump starts, your motor is fine and you have a timer problem — which
is a different and cheaper repair.

Automation panels have their own relays, and a relay that has welded or failed
open presents exactly like a dead motor.

### 3. Whether power is reaching the motor.

**What to check:** voltage at the motor's terminal board, with a meter.

This is the step that splits the diagnosis cleanly in two. Voltage present means
the supply chain is fine and the fault is inside the motor. No voltage means the
motor is innocent and the problem is upstream — breaker, timer, relay, or wiring.

If you're not comfortable metering a live circuit, this is a sensible point to
call someone. It's also the point where a technician will start, which is worth
knowing before you pay for the visit.

### 4. The start capacitor.

**What to check:** the capacitor, usually under a cover on top of the motor or in
the end housing.

**Good:** a flat top, clean sides, terminals free of corrosion.

**Bad:** a domed or bulging top, split casing, leaked residue, or a burnt smell.
A visibly swollen capacitor is a confirmed diagnosis — you don't need a meter to
call it.

A capacitor can also fail with no outward sign at all, which is why the humming
symptom matters more than the appearance. If the motor hums and the shaft turns
freely by hand, the capacitor is the leading suspect regardless of how it looks.

<SafetyWarning>
  A start capacitor stores an electrical charge after the power is off, and it
  can hold enough to injure you. Cut power at the breaker, confirm it's dead,
  and discharge the capacitor across its terminals with an insulated tool before
  touching them. Never bridge the terminals with a bare screwdriver and your
  hand on the shaft. If you aren't confident doing this safely, stop here and
  call someone — this is a genuinely reasonable place to pay for the visit.
</SafetyWarning>

### 5. Whether the shaft turns at all.

**What to check:** breaker off, lid off, turn the motor shaft by hand.

**Good:** it turns freely with light resistance.

**Bad:** it won't move, or grinds. A seized shaft means the motor tries to start
into a locked rotor, which is why some humming motors have a mechanical cause
rather than an electrical one. If the shaft is rough but turning, you have a
bearing problem as well — see [pool pump loud
noise](/equipment/pool-pump-loud-noise), where that fault gets its own
diagnosis.

<UncommonTip>
  **A pump that dies in the afternoon and works again by evening isn't an
  intermittent electrical fault. It's the thermal overload, and the pattern is
  the diagnosis.**

  Pool pump motors carry a built-in thermal overload — a switch that cuts power
  when the windings get too hot and closes again once they cool. It's a
  protection device, and when it operates it is working correctly.

  The signature is the timing. A motor that stops and won't restart for roughly
  20 to 30 minutes, then runs normally again, is almost certainly cycling on
  that overload. That reset interval is characteristic, and it's why the fault
  seems to "fix itself" every time you come back to look at it.

  The trap is treating the symptom as electrical. People replace breakers,
  rewire timers and chase loose connections for weeks, because every time they
  test it the pump runs. **The overload isn't the fault. It's the alarm.**
  Something is making the motor overheat, and the usual culprits are failing
  bearings adding drag, airflow blocked at the motor's cooling vents, a motor
  running in direct sun with no shade, low supply voltage, or cavitation making
  it work against a starved suction line.

  So when the pattern is *runs, stops, returns after half an hour*, stop testing
  the electrics. Ask what's making it hot — that's the actual repair, and it's
  usually cheaper than anything you were about to replace.
</UncommonTip>

## Parts you may need

In the order they actually fail:

- **Start capacitor** — the most common, and among the cheapest parts on the
  pad. Must match microfarad rating and voltage exactly; close is not close
  enough.
- **Mechanical timer mechanism or trippers** — worn dogs and drifted clocks.
- **Automation relay** — fails open or welds closed, mimicking a dead motor.
- **Centrifugal switch** — inside the motor, and the reason some motors hum with
  a perfectly good capacitor.
- **Motor** — genuinely last, and only after the above are ruled out.

## What it costs to have done

- **Breaker or timer** — a short visit, and the tech will find it in minutes.
- **Capacitor replacement** — parts are cheap, labor is brief. The classic case
  of a small bill preventing a large one.
- **Motor replacement** — the outcome worth avoiding, and the one that gets
  quoted first when nobody has metered anything. If a quote arrives without
  someone having checked voltage at the terminal board and looked at the
  capacitor, ask why.

That's the honest framing: a fair technician works this same list, and the value
of doing it yourself is mostly knowing whether the quote you received matches
the fault you have.

## When to stop and call someone

- **The breaker trips the moment the pump starts.** That's a short or a ground
  fault. Repeated resetting is how equipment fires start.
- **Any burning smell, scorched insulation, or discolored terminals.** Stop.
- **You'd be metering a live circuit and you aren't sure.** No shame in it —
  this is the specific step that justifies a call-out.
- **The shaft is seized.** The motor is coming apart either way.
- **The pump is wired without a visible disconnect**, or the wiring looks
  improvised. Get that assessed before you work on anything downstream.

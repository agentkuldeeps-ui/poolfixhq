Search this and you'll get one list of causes. That list can't be right, because
"pool cleaner" describes three different machines that share almost nothing but a
job title.

Here's how different. These are two manufacturers' own troubleshooting lists for
the same complaint — a cleaner that won't move.

| Suction-side (Hayward) | Pressure-side (Polaris) |
|---|---|
| Enough water flow past the turbine | Feed hose within 6" of the pool's farthest point |
| Vacuum in the hose — about 1" per hose section | Brass insert on the single side wheel |
| Shoes worn, or wrong for the pool surface | Thrust jet position — 7 o'clock from the factory |
| Debris jammed between bearings and turbine | Drive chain disengaged |
| Drivetrain play over ¼" | Back-up valve cycling; wheel speed 30–36 RPM |

**Not one cause appears on both lists.** So before anything else, work out which
machine you own.

## Cleaner stopped moving? Find the type first

Follow the hose — that single move settles it.

**Suction-side.** Connects to a skimmer or a dedicated suction port. The pump
pulls water through the cleaner, and that flow both drives it and carries debris
back to your pump basket or filter. It is completely dependent on your pool's
circulation.

**Pressure-side.** Connects to a return-side line, often with its own booster
pump. Water is pushed to it, jets drive it around, and debris goes into a bag on
the cleaner rather than into your filter.

**Robotic.** Plugs into an electrical outlet, has no hose to the equipment pad,
and filters into its own onboard basket. It runs whether your pump is on or off.
Nothing about your filter, your flow or your valves affects it.

That last one matters more than it sounds. **If you own a robot, everything about
your pool's circulation is irrelevant to this problem** — and a great deal of the
advice you'll find is about circulation.

## Suction cleaners

The machine is driven by the water your pump pulls through it, so it has a flow
requirement — but the useful part is that Hayward gives you numbers rather than
"make sure flow is adequate."

**Flow.** There has to be enough moving past the turbine. Hayward's field check is
roughly **one inch of vacuum per section of hose**, and if you're using a flow
gauge, to set it **at least halfway between minimum and maximum.** Note the shape
of that: a window, not a maximum. More is not better.

If flow is short, the cause is usually upstream rather than in the cleaner —
start with [high filter pressure](/equipment/high-filter-pressure) and, if the
returns are weak too, [weak flow from the returns](/equipment/low-pool-flow). A
cleaner that stalls while the pump also struggles to hold prime points at the
suction side instead — [pump not priming](/equipment/pump-not-priming).

**The hose itself.** A cracked or perished cleaner hose is one of the most common
suction-side air leaks on a pool, and it produces two symptoms at once — a
sluggish cleaner and [air bubbles in the returns](/equipment/air-in-pool-lines).
If you have both, suspect the hose before the machine.

**Traction.** Covered below. Check this before you dismantle anything.

**Obstruction.** Hayward's instruction is to remove the consumer inspection plate
at the bottom of the cleaner and look for a rock, twig or large piece of debris
stuck between the bearings and the turbine. This is common and free to fix.

**Drivetrain wear.** Hold the left and right pods and firmly try to rock them.
**More than a quarter inch of play means parts need replacing** — that's a
measurable test rather than a judgement call, which is unusually useful.

## Pressure cleaners

A different machine with a different fault list. Polaris's checks for the
Vac-Sweep 3900 Sport:

- **Feed hose length** — within six inches, longer or shorter, of the distance to
  the farthest point of the pool. Too short and it can't reach; too long and it
  tangles.
- **The brass insert on the single side wheel**, if it has come loose.
- **Thrust jet position** — the factory setting is 7 o'clock. This is the kind of
  detail that gets moved during a clean and never moved back.
- **Drive chain** — check whether it has disengaged.
- **Back-up valve** — lift it out of the water and watch the jet to confirm it's
  cycling.
- **Wheel speed** — should run 30 to 36 RPM. Mark one tire and count.

Those are model-specific figures. Check yours against its own manual rather than
assuming they carry across the range.

## Robotic cleaners

Neither manufacturer source here covers robots, and we're not going to improvise
diagnostics for a machine we haven't got documentation on.

What can be said with confidence is the boundary: a robot is self-contained. It
has its own motor, its own filter and its own power supply, so your pump, filter
pressure, valve positions and water chemistry have no bearing on whether it
moves. If a robot has stopped, the fault is in the unit, its cable or its power
supply — and the checks in the rest of this article don't apply to it.

<UncommonTip>
  **A suction cleaner's grip is a consumable part, and the right one depends on
  what your pool is made of.**

  The pads a suction cleaner walks on — its "shoes" — wear out, and they're
  chosen by surface. Hayward is specific: **standard or slotted shoes for plaster
  and Pebble-Tec, cork for vinyl and fiberglass, and ceramic for tile.**

  Fit the wrong material and the cleaner slips, stalls, or spins on the spot. It
  looks exactly like a seized drivetrain or a flow problem, and it gets diagnosed
  as one — because nothing about the symptom suggests a compatibility issue with
  a part most owners don't know exists.

  Where it bites is predictable once you see it. A pool gets resurfaced from
  vinyl to plaster and the cleaner that worked for years suddenly won't climb. A
  used cleaner arrives wearing shoes for someone else's pool. A generic
  replacement part gets fitted because it was cheaper and it physically fits.

  None of those look like parts problems. All of them are.

  It's also near the bottom of the cost list, which is the argument for checking
  it early rather than late. Flip the cleaner over and look at what it's standing
  on before you open it up.
</UncommonTip>

## Why it comes back

**Shoes wear — that's their function.** A cleaner that gradually loses climbing
ability over a season is usually reporting worn traction rather than a dying
motor.

**Suction cleaners follow your filter's cycle.** If the cleaner slows as the
filter loads and recovers after a backwash, the cleaner is fine and the flow
margin is thin.

**Debris finds the turbine repeatedly** if the pool is under trees. That's the
job working as intended, not a fault, though it argues for checking the
inspection plate as routine rather than as diagnosis.

**A cleaner that stops working the pool doesn't only mean a dirty floor.** Poor
coverage leaves dead zones where circulation is weakest, and those are where
blooms start — [green pool water](/problems/green-pool-water) covers what
follows.

<SafetyWarning>
  **Isolate power before servicing a robotic cleaner.** Unplug it at the outlet
  rather than at the unit, and never open a power supply.

  **Never run a suction cleaner on a pool with a damaged, loose or missing main
  drain cover, and don't remove a cover to improve suction.** Those covers are
  safety components.

## When to stop and call someone

- **More than a quarter inch of play in the pods.** Hayward routes this to an
  authorized dealer, and it's the point where parts costs start approaching a
  new unit.
- **A pressure cleaner that fails its checks with a booster pump involved.**
  Booster pump problems are pump problems, and they're wired separately.
- **A robot that won't power up.** Electrical, not serviceable at home.
- **Any damaged or missing drain cover, at any point.** Stop using the cleaner
  and get it addressed before anything else. Start at [pool
  repair](/pool-repair).

Worth being blunt about the economics, because this is where cleaners differ from
the rest of the equipment pad: **a cleaner is often not worth repairing.** Once
you're buying a drivetrain rebuild for an older unit, the parts and labor can
approach the price of a replacement, and the replacement comes with a warranty.
Shoes, a jammed turbine or a moved thrust jet are absolutely worth fixing. A worn
drivetrain on a machine that's had a decade of sun usually isn't. Saying so costs
us the parts referral, and it's still the honest read.

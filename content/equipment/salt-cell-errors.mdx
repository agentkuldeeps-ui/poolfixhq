A salt cell throwing an error is usually reporting a *condition* — salt is low,
water is cold, flow is short — rather than announcing its own death. The
indicators are diagnostics, and most of them point away from the cell entirely.

The exception is the one that gets read as a death sentence and often isn't.

## What each indicator means

These are Hayward AquaRite behaviors, quoted from their manual. Other brands use
different lights and different thresholds, so treat this as the pattern rather
than as universal.

| Indicator | Hayward's stated meaning |
|---|---|
| **Power** | The unit has input power |
| **Generating** | Steady during normal operation. **Flashing: water is too hot or cold to operate** |
| **Check Salt** | **Flashing:** salt is low, below 2,700 ppm, generating at low efficiency. **Steady:** salt too low, unit has shut down |
| **Inspect Cell** | Flashing means *either* cell efficiency is reduced *or* it's time for scheduled inspection |
| **No Flow** | The flow switch has detected no water flowing; the unit has stopped generating |

Read that Inspect Cell row again — it's doing two jobs, and that matters more
than anything else on this page.

## Salt, and why you should test before adding

Hayward's ideal range is **2,700–3,400 ppm, with 3,200 optimal.** Below 2,700 the
Check Salt light flashes and output drops; lower still and the unit stops.

The important habit: **test independently before adding salt.** The number on the
display is what the system measures, and a scaled or aging cell can measure
badly. Adding salt against a wrong reading is how pools end up over-salted — and
salt doesn't evaporate. The only way down is dilution, which means draining and
refilling water you just paid to treat.

Salt is dosed per gallon, so the figure that matters is your actual water volume
rather than the number on the pool's paperwork. Work it out with the [pool volume
calculator](/tools/pool-volume-calculator) before adding anything, and add in
increments with a re-test between them.

## When the error is really a flow problem

**No Flow** is the indicator most likely to send you looking at the wrong
component. The flow switch isn't seeing enough water — which is not the same as
the pump being off.

Work it upstream: filter pressure against its clean baseline first, then valve
positions. [High filter pressure](/equipment/high-filter-pressure) covers reading
the gauge properly, and [weak flow from the returns](/equipment/low-pool-flow)
sorts a restriction by which side of the pump it's on.

One installation detail worth knowing: Hayward specifies at least a 12-inch
straight pipe run upstream of the flow switch. A switch mounted too close to a
fitting can read flow inaccurately, which produces a No Flow error on a system
that's actually moving water fine.

There's a safety dimension to this one that belongs to a different page — running
a cell without adequate flow is what manufacturers warn about most sharply. [Air
bubbles in the returns](/equipment/air-in-pool-lines) covers that, including the
wiring requirement that stops a cell energizing when the pump is off.

## When nothing is wrong

**Cold water.** Hayward's AquaRite "automatically stops generating when the pool
water temperatures drops below 50ºF," and a flashing Generating light means the
water is outside its operating range. A cell that quits in spring or late fall is
usually behaving exactly as designed.

That does leave a real problem though: the pool still needs sanitizing while the
cell is idle. Cold water slows chlorine demand, it doesn't remove it, so plan to
supplement rather than assume the pool is fine until it warms up.

<UncommonTip>
  **"Inspect Cell" is a timer as often as it's a diagnosis — and cells are one of
  the most expensive parts on the pool.**

  Hayward's manual is explicit that a flashing Inspect Cell indicator "signifies
  that **either** the cell efficiency is reduced **or** that it is time for
  regularly scheduled cell inspection." Their cleaning guide is blunter about the
  second half: it's "a built-in reminder to inspect and clean the cell every 500
  hrs."

  So one light carries two unrelated messages. One says something may be wrong.
  The other says a counter reached a number.

  Owners overwhelmingly read it the first way. The light comes on, the cell is
  assumed dead, and a replacement gets ordered — for a part that can cost as much
  as everything else on the equipment pad combined. Sometimes the old cell was
  scaled and would have come back with a fifteen-minute acid soak.

  The light means **look at the cell.** It does not tell you what you'll find.

  How to tell them apart: inspect and clean it, clear the reminder, and watch
  whether it returns early. Scale on the plates explains reduced efficiency and
  cleaning fixes it. A genuinely clean cell that flags again soon is telling you
  something different — and *that's* the point at which replacement is a real
  conversation.

## Cleaning the cell

Only if inspection finds scale. Hayward's own interval is roughly every three
months of operation or 500 hours, and the instruction is to clean *if necessary*
— not on schedule regardless.

<SafetyWarning>
  Cell cleaning uses muriatic acid, and this is the one genuinely
  owner-serviceable step on this page.

  Hayward's ratio is **4:1 water to acid** — one gallon of water to one quart of
  acid. Fill the cell and soak about 15 minutes.

  **ALWAYS ADD ACID TO WATER — NEVER ADD WATER TO ACID.** Adding water to acid
  can cause it to spit violently.

  Wear rubber gloves and appropriate eye protection. Work outdoors, away from
  anything the solution can splash onto, and never mix acid with any other pool
  chemical — particularly not chlorine products.

  If any of that reads as more than you want to take on, this is a sensible job
  to hand to a technician. It's cheap work.
</SafetyWarning>

Don't over-clean. Acid removes scale, and Hayward notes that scale "lessens the
effectiveness and life span of the salt cell" — but repeated soaking of a cell
that didn't need it works on the plates as well as the deposits. Inspect, then
decide.

## Why it comes back

**Scale returns if the water chemistry that caused it hasn't changed.** A cell
that scales up every few months is downstream of saturation chemistry, not a
faulty cell — [milky white pool water](/problems/milky-white-pool-water) covers
what drives calcium out of solution and onto the nearest hot surface, which is
exactly what a cell is.

**Flow problems recur on the filter's cycle.** If No Flow tracks your backwash
schedule, the margin is thin rather than the switch faulty.

**Cells genuinely wear out.** They have a finite life and output declines with
age. That's real — the argument here is only that the light alone doesn't prove
you've reached it.

## When to stop and call someone

- **A cleaned, scale-free cell that still flags Inspect Cell early.** That's the
  conversation about replacement, now with evidence.
- **No Flow that survives a clean filter, confirmed valve positions and a
  correctly located flow switch.** The switch itself may have failed.
- **Any doubt about whether the cell can energize while the pump is off.** That's
  an electrical and safety issue — see the bubbles article, then get it checked.
- **Salt readings you can't reconcile.** If the display and an independent test
  disagree badly, don't dose against either until someone resolves it. [Pool
  repair](/pool-repair) is the starting point if it needs hands on it.

The expensive mistake on this page is buying a cell because a light came on.
It's frequently the single costliest part on a residential pool, and the light
that prompts the purchase is one Hayward defines as *possibly just a reminder*.
Clean it, watch it, then decide. Telling you that costs us the largest affiliate
sale in the equipment cluster.
